## meaning

Displaying His sacred form sparkling like lightning, His broad arms, palms which subdued the elephant (Kuvalayāpīḍa), eyes, lips and matching ear-ornaments hanging low and sparkling below the garland of tulasī exuding fragrance of its own, He not only took away my character, my femininity, my mind and my bangles, but also made me His vassal and went away saying that His residence was Śrīraṅgam with copious water and groves exuding fragrance from the blossoming golden-coloured ceruṇḍi flowers.

## purport

As Bhagavān continues to depart, Parākāla Nāyaki repeatedly expresses the anguish of separation. The vision of the Lord remains vividly before her eyes, making His absence even more unbearable. He had descended before her, revealing Himself in all His splendour, with an effulgence shining like a flash of lightning. Having once beheld such beauty, she finds herself unable to endure the thought of being separated from Him.

She recalls His broad shoulders and powerful arms, the very arms with which He subdued the mighty elephant Kuvalayāpīḍa during His Kṛṣṇāvatāra. Yet, alongside this display of strength, she remembers the extraordinary tenderness of His form. His eyes and lips are soft and delicate like freshly blossomed lotus petals, capable of melting even the hardest of hearts. His Makara-kuṇḍalas sway gracefully as He moves, adding to the charm of His appearance. The divine fragrance emanating from His person mingles with the sweet scent of the tulasī garland adorning Him, enchanting all who come near. Faced with such an irresistibly beautiful form, Parākāla Nāyaki wonders how anyone could possibly remain apart from Him.

Overcome with emotion, she questions the Lord Himself. Why should He move away from her when He was the very one who had stolen her heart? He had come to her uninvited, captivated her mind, and drawn all her thoughts towards Himself. Having awakened such love within her, how could He now depart and leave her behind?

Āḻvār illustrates this sentiment through a striking analogy. It is as though a clever robber enters a house, distracts the children with attractive toys, gathers all the valuables, and then even persuades the householder himself to carry away the stolen goods. In the same manner, the Lord entered her heart, captivated her through His beauty and grace, took possession of her mind, thoughts, and affections, and then departed, leaving her utterly helpless.

Yet, as her emotions settle, Parākāla Nāyaki arrives at a deeper realization. After all, she belongs exclusively to Bhagavān. The soul is His possession by its very nature. If He chooses to claim her mind, her heart, and her very self, He is merely taking what already belongs to Him. Therefore, she cannot truly accuse Him of theft, for the owner cannot steal his own property. Recognizing this truth, her complaint gradually transforms into loving acceptance.

Remembering His final words, she takes comfort in the promise He had given her. He had assured her that they would meet again at the sacred island embraced by the waters of the Kāveri, namely Śrīraṅgam. Thus, even amidst the pain of separation, Parākāla Nāyaki finds a measure of solace in the hope of reunion. Her thoughts turn towards Śrīraṅgam, where the Lord awaits, and where the anguish of separation will ultimately be resolved through the joy of His presence.
