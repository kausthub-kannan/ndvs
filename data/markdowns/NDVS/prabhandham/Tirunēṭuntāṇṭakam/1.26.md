## meaning

Six-footed little bee staying happily on the flower, supremely contented, along with your female companion putting into each other's mouth the honey at the time of blossoming in the honey-rich flower garden! I prostrate to you. Go right today to the Lord (Amaruviyappan) staying at lovely Aḷundūr, the Lord of the Nityasūris and the Lord who mixed with the cows and grazed them, and see that you speak a word that a certain dame is desiring Him.

## purport

The heroine had experienced immense joy during her union with the Lord. After He departed, the companions who had been guarding the entrance approached her and eagerly questioned her about the encounter. They wished to know how the Lord appeared and what He had spoken to her. The heroine answered these questions in the earlier pāsurams, describing His incomparable beauty, divine splendour, and the words He had spoken. She then explained that Garuḍa, eager to serve his Master, hastened the Lord's departure and carried Him away before she could fully enjoy His presence.

When the companions asked why she had not requested Him to remain with her, the heroine explained that He was surrounded on all sides by the Nityasūris. In the midst of that glorious assembly, she found no opportunity to speak freely to Him. Later, she described how the Lord overwhelmed her by revealing His majestic splendour, captivated her completely, took possession of her heart, and reduced her to His willing servant. Having recounted all these events, neither the companions nor the heroine have anything further to discuss. The exhilaration of union gives way to the heaviness of separation, and a profound sense of despondency settles upon her.

Reflecting upon the earlier conversation, the heroine arrives at a new resolution. Since she can no longer speak to the Lord directly, she decides to send an emissary to convey her message of longing. Yet the question immediately arises: whom should she choose for such a delicate mission? The scriptures provide examples that caution against relying merely upon intelligence or eloquence. Kṛṣṇa Himself went as a messenger to the court of Duryodhana, yet His mission did not avert the great war. By contrast, Hanumān, though only a monkey in outward appearance, successfully fulfilled his mission and brought hope to Sītā in her distress. Thus, success in divine service depends not merely upon ability but upon fitness for the task and the Lord's grace.

As she contemplates this, her attention falls upon a bee happily sporting with its mate among the flowers of the garden. Seeing it immersed in delight, she chooses it as her messenger. She praises the bee and entreats it to carry her message to the Lord. In the traditional interpretation, the bee assumes the role of an Ācārya, whose sacred function is to unite the soul with Bhagavān. Just as the Ācārya carries the yearning soul towards its divine destination, the bee is asked to serve as the intermediary between the heroine and her beloved Lord.

Addressing it as the "six-footed one," the heroine employs a term that is rich in symbolic meaning. While bees naturally possess six legs, the commentators discern a deeper significance. Śrī Parāśara Bhaṭṭar beautifully explains that the three pairs of feet symbolize the feet of the Ācārya, his wife, and his son, all of whom deserve reverence. Once the bee successfully accomplishes its mission, the heroine imagines placing those sacred feet upon her head and rejoicing in gratitude.

A further question arises. The Lord had already told the heroine that His abode was at Śrīraṅgam. Why then does she ask the bee to seek Him at Aḻundūr? The commentators offer a charming explanation. The heroine assumes that the Lord would have reached Śrīraṅgam, but upon arriving there, He would have found even that glorious abode devoid of joy without the presence of a true devotee such as herself. Unable to bear the separation any more than she can, He would already have begun His journey back towards her. Thus, she directs the bee to Aḻundūr, presuming that the Lord will be found there on His return.

The pāsuram therefore beautifully combines the themes of separation, longing, and the indispensable role of the Ācārya. Even amidst the sorrow of separation, the heroine remains sustained by hope, convinced that the Lord who captured her heart cannot remain content without reclaiming her and that the messenger she sends will ultimately bring about their reunion.
