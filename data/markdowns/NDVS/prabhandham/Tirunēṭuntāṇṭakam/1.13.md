## meaning

With tear drops trickling down her twin breasts, and feeling weary, she asks her parrot to repeat by prompting it, such as, "Lord who protected from hailstorm by lifting the hill!" "Lord Uraga at lovely and highly desired Kāñcī!" "Lord who embraced the tender arms (of Sītā) by breaking the bow!" "The Master who is reposing in Vēṅka!" "The Lord who vanquished the wrestlers at one time and made them powerless!" and, "My youthful Lord with palms that split open (the mouth) of the horse Keśī."

## purport

The mother observed that the repeated chanting of the Lord's names had a soothing effect on the heroine's distressed condition. Hoping to bring her further comfort, she brought the heroine's pet parrot and placed it beside her, expecting it to repeat the divine names as it had been taught. However, the parrot remained silent. Traditional commentators explain this silence through the analogy of a disciple who, even after receiving instruction from an Ācārya, does not display his learning independently in the presence of the teacher. Out of humility and reverence, the disciple speaks only when prompted.

Accordingly, the parrot waited in silence until the heroine herself addressed it. Once encouraged by its mistress, it began to repeat the sacred names of the Lord, thereby offering comfort to the very one who had taught it. The scene beautifully illustrates the reciprocal nature of devotion, where even a simple creature becomes an instrument of divine remembrance and consolation.

A distinctive feature of Tirumaṅgai Āḻvār's devotional experience is his profound fulfillment in the Arcāvatāra. While the other Āḻvārs frequently celebrate the Lord's transcendence in Paramapada, His accessibility through various avatāras, and His presence in Arcā forms, Tirumaṅgai Āḻvār displays a particularly intense attachment to the Arcā manifestations found in the Divya Deśams. For him, the Lord enshrined in the temple is not different from the Lord who appeared in the great avatāras described in the scriptures.

In this pāsuram, Āḻvār emphasizes the essential oneness of the Lord in both Arcā and Avatāra. While standing before the Arcā form, he recalls the deeds performed during Kṛṣṇa's earthly incarnation and addresses the Lord directly as the very same Kṛṣṇa who protected His devotees. Thus, there is no distinction between the deity worshipped in the temple and the Lord who enacted divine sports in ages past.

Āḻvār specifically recalls the Govardhana episode through the words, "You warded off the hailstones by lifting a stone aloft." Here, Govardhana Hill is deliberately referred to as merely a stone in order to highlight the effortless nature of Kṛṣṇa's act. What appears to the world as a mighty mountain is, from the perspective of the omnipotent Lord, no more difficult to lift than a small stone.

The episode also reveals the wondrous and inscrutable ways of Bhagavān. When torrential rains and hailstones threatened the inhabitants of Gokula, the Lord chose to protect them by using another stone, Govardhana itself, as their shelter. In this sense, He countered stone with stone, much as one might speak of fighting fire with fire. Through this delightful recollection, Āḻvār celebrates both the Lord's infinite power and His affectionate concern for those who seek refuge in Him.
