## english_scripts

alam purinda neḍun taḍakkai amarar vēndan
añjiṟai ppuḻ tani ppāgan avuṇarkkenṟum
śalam purindaṅgaruḻ illā ttanmai yāḻan
tān uganda ūr ellām tan tāḻ pāḍi
nilam parandu varum kaluzi ppeṇṇai īrtta
neḍu vēygaḻ paḍu muttam unda undi
pulam parandu pon viḻaikkum poygai vēli
pūṅgōvalūr tozudum pōdu neñjē !

## meaning

The Lord of the Nityasūris, the unique Rider of Garuḍa of lovely wings, who, by nature, has hands far-flung and generous to satiety (towards His devotees), does not grace, out of anger, the rākṣasas (inimical people). Mind! Let us prostrate, eulogizing His feet through all towns relished by Him on the way, lovely Kōvalūr surrounded by tanks and with spread-out fields growing precious crops, where pearl-like droplets in mire oozing out from tall bamboo trees, brought and thrown about in the fields by the overflowing water of river Pennai are discarded (by the farmers).

## purport

Having given a magnificent description of the Lord in His Trivikrama avatāra in the previous pāsuram, Āḻvār need not lament that he was not present during that divine age to behold the Lord with his own eyes. The same Lord continues to reside at Tirukkōvaḷūr in the very form in which He manifested during that avatāra, making Himself available to devotees born in later generations. Thus, the blessings of witnessing Trivikrama are not confined to those who lived during the avatāra itself, but remain accessible to all who approach Him with devotion.

Āḻvār's mention of the Nityasūris and Garuḍa highlights the transcendental nature of Nārāyaṇa. In Paramapada, the Lord has already bestowed the highest gift upon the Nityasūris, namely Himself, and there are no hostile or inimical beings there from whom protection is required. Therefore, references to the Nityasūris and Garuḍa serve to emphasize the Lord's divine majesty while simultaneously drawing attention to His extraordinary accessibility through His avatāras and arcā manifestations in this world.

The Lord who transcends all realms willingly descends among humanity so that ordinary beings may approach Him, worship Him, and experience His grace. Reflecting upon this boundless accessibility, Āḻvār turns to his own mind and urges it to undertake a sacred pilgrimage through the Lord's temple towns. Just as a weary traveller carefully chooses hospitable resting places while avoiding barren and inhospitable regions, the devotee should seek refuge in the Divya Deśams where the Lord graciously resides. Journeying from one sacred abode to another, singing His glories along the way, the mind becomes steadily absorbed in divine contemplation and progresses towards its ultimate goal of eternal service to the Lord.
