## meaning

After listening to the sonorous names of the Lord of Śrī at Tiruttaṅkal and Tiruvēṅka, effulgent like the rising Sun, the cloud-hued Lord at Kuruṅkuḍi, who is supreme and beyond the three types of souls (baddha, mukta and nitya) in the three worlds, the insatiable Nectar beyond scrutiny, the immaculate Lord at Śrīraṅgam, the Lord in the hearts of pure souls, the Lord who dispels ignorance like the darkness from the light of the lamp and who is pleasingly lustrous like emerald, by the obedient parrot, she folded her hands and prostrated, saying "Welcome; I am profited by bringing you up."

## purport

As explained in the previous pāsuram, the parrot began reciting the names of the Lord after being prompted by the heroine. Hearing those sacred names emerge from the bird she had lovingly nurtured and instructed, the heroine experiences immense joy. She feels that her efforts in raising and teaching the parrot have finally borne fruit, for it has now become an instrument for glorifying Bhagavān.

The phrase *"kaikkūppi vaṇaṅgināḷē"* indicates that the heroine folded her hands in reverence and saluted the parrot. At first glance, this may appear surprising. Why should the heroine offer such respect to a mere parrot, especially one that may be regarded as her own disciple? The commentators explain that the significance lies not in the bird itself, but in what it is doing. By reciting the divine names of the Lord, the parrot assumes the role of a Bhāgavata, one who glorifies Bhagavān, and thereby becomes worthy of veneration.

The deeper teaching conveyed through this episode is that all Bhāgavatas who recite and celebrate the names of the Lord deserve reverence, irrespective of their age, social position, learning, or status. The greatness of a devotee is measured not by worldly qualifications but by devotion to Bhagavān and engagement in His remembrance.

Traditional history provides many examples illustrating this principle. One such incident concerns Mahāpūrṇar, the revered Ācārya of Rāmānuja. On one occasion, Mahāpūrṇar prostrated before a group of Śrīvaiṣṇavas who were returning from their ablutions in the Kāveri under the leadership of his own disciple, Rāmānuja. Although Rāmānuja was his disciple, Mahāpūrṇar saw in that assembly not merely individuals, but devotees of the Lord who were adorned with devotion and service. Therefore, he offered them his respects without hesitation.

In the same spirit, the heroine bows before the parrot. Her reverence is directed not towards the bird as an individual creature, but towards the sacred act of chanting the Lord's names. Through this beautiful episode, Āḻvār teaches that anyone who becomes a vehicle for the glorification of Bhagavān deserves honour and respect. The pāsuram thus highlights the exalted position of the Bhāgavata and the humility with which true devotees regard those who engage in the remembrance and praise of the Lord.
