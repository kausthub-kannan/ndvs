## meaning

It is said that the cloud-hued Lord, together with the effulgence of Lakṣmī, shone with a white body in a gigantic form as the Tortoise at the time when the nectar was obtained from the Milk Ocean (in Kṛta Yuga), and that He was of a red hue in Tretā Yuga. The great Lord of blue complexion (in Kali Yuga) cannot be conceived of as possessing any single form, nature, or color. Apart from praising Him continuously, age after age, through mere imagination, has anyone truly seen the dark-bodied, red-eyed Lord as he has seen Him?

## purport

In the first pāsuram, the Lord revealed to Āḻvār the futility of attachment to the body and the individual self, as well as the inadequacy of remaining in kaivalya. He also granted him the vision of His own incomparable beauty. In the second pāsuram, He revealed that He alone, and none other, is the sole Master of all. Having received these revelations, Āḻvār now stands like a chaste wife wholly dedicated to her husband. In this pāsuram, Āḻvār declares that he beholds the Lord in His true nature and in the full splendour of His divine form, unlike others who, throughout the ages, perceive Him only according to the limitations imposed by their surroundings and mental dispositions, shaped as they are by the three guṇas of sattva, rajas, and tamas.

During Kṛta Yuga, sattva predominates, and therefore people perceive the Lord as white, the colour traditionally associated with sattva. In Tretā Yuga, rajas becomes dominant, and the Lord is seen in red, the colour corresponding to that quality. In Dvāpara Yuga, where rajas and tamas exist in a mixed proportion, the perceived colour is understood to be intermediate in nature. In Kali Yuga, tamas predominates, and its associated colour is black. Yet, since people of this age are generally not inclined towards God and are unable to perceive Him as He truly is, the Lord remains in His natural hue, the deep blue complexion of Nilamegha Śyāma, manifesting in His own intrinsic splendour.

Āḻvār also beautifully extols the divinity of Mahālakṣmī through the phrase *kaḍalamutam koṇḍa kālam*. When the Milk Ocean was churned, not only did the devas obtain nectar, but the Lord Himself gained the supreme nectar in the form of Mahālakṣmī, who emerged from those sacred waters. Āḻvār, in turn, partakes of that same nectar through his loving glorification of the Lord and His divine consort. Thus, the episode becomes not merely an account of cosmic events, but a celebration of the inseparable union of the Divine Couple and the bliss experienced by the devotee who contemplates them.
