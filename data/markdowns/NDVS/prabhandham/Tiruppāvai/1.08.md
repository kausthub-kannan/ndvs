## meaning

Day is breaking in the east. The buffaloes are released for feed; they are freely making their way to the small meadow. The other girls are proceeding to the place of the ritual, as though their whole purpose lies in reaching there. We have stopped them, and we come here to invite you; we stand in your courtyard. O woman of intense longing! Wake up and join us. Let us praise Kṛṣṇa's attributes and launch upon our ritual; let us obtain 'paṟai' which is the essential implement required for our rite. Let us adore Kṛṣṇa. He has killed the demon Keśī by splitting him into two; He has destroyed the wrestlers Cāṇūra and Muṣṭika; He is the God of all gods, God of all the celestials. When we go to Him and render Him worship, He will feel concerned that we have taken the trouble of going to Him, and will hasten to consider all the aspects of our situation and grant us what we want.

## purport

Āṇḍāḷ continues to wake the gopī who is not agreeing that dawn has arisen. She quotes that the day has broken and requests her to wake up; the sun is rising from the east, spreading its rays everywhere. These rays are similar to the gopī's face. As soon as night occurs, these gopīs run to meet Kṛṣṇa and enjoy listening to His veṇugāna. But while immersed in the experience, there is slight worry in them; they keep looking east, checking if dawn breaks. Each time they rotate their heads, their faces glow like the moon, emitting rays of spiritual love. But here, the gopī was unable to participate in the act and is waiting to meet the Gopāla of Vṛndāvana.

Āṇḍāḷ awakens her by saying the buffaloes have been let out to graze in the small meadows, similar to how Toṇḍaraḍippoḍi Āḷvār tries to wake Raṅganātha. Unlike cows, buffaloes are left to graze twice - near dawn and later morning. At dawn, they graze in nearby meadows and later are sent far off. Hence, by saying the buffaloes are grazing near the small meadows and will soon return to their sheds, Āṇḍāḷ indicates the risen dawn. On the level of spiritual knowledge, these buffaloes represent the guṇas rajas and tamas. Rajas induces passion for material pleasures, and tamas brings ignorance which forces one away from spiritual knowledge. Internally, even if we reduce such guṇas' impact, due to vāsanās carried from several previous births, we are forced to act upon them. Only after dawn are buffaloes sent far off; similarly, only when we cling to Bhagavān can we ward off such guṇas and adopt sattva alone, which brings desire for spiritual knowledge and peace of mind. If buffaloes wake at dawn to graze upon mere grass, then devotees of Kṛṣṇa like the gopī too need to wake at dawn, going towards the objective of enjoying the Supreme Self. Now the gopī wants reassurance that such people have gathered here to meet this objective.

To assure the gopī, Āṇḍāḷ beautifully quotes that other devotees and gopīs have assembled here waiting for her. She particularly addresses why devotees try to meet Kṛṣṇa even when attainment may be uncertain. Her justification is wonderful - she emphasizes that though we may or may not attain Kṛṣṇa, the journey itself provides immense bliss. Moreover, we don't desire such great bliss but only to serve Bhagavān. That itself is part of the journey, hence we are happy irrespective of the result. When Akrūra was ordered by Kaṃsa to fetch the two gopālas of Vraja - Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma - he was in bliss thinking that merely going to Vṛndāvana itself was the goal. When Vibhīṣaṇa was banished from Laṅkā, he went to the northern shore where Rāma stayed. Vālmīki specifically quotes "Uttaram tīra māsādya khasthaḥ eva vyatiṣṭhata" - he stood in the sky above the shore. Using a continuous word for the verb 'stand', Vālmīki seems to indicate that he stood watching Rāma, merely happy about that fact. Similarly, Āṇḍāḷ says "vandu ninṟōm." The gopī questions Āṇḍāḷ about why they have gathered to call her. Āṇḍāḷ responds that she knows well the gopī desires Kṛṣṇa more than life itself, as does Kṛṣṇa desire her. After knowing this, how could they not call her? She speaks similar to Sītā Devī showing sympathy for those under Rāvaṇa's cruel regime. Hence, Āṇḍāḷ calls upon the gopī to participate in the nombu and receive Kṛṣṇa's blessings to obtain the paṟai (which also refers to divine service).

When the gopī asks why Kṛṣṇa would deign them paṟai, Āṇḍāḷ responds by narrating His līlās. She describes how He split the mouth of the demon Keśī, who took the form of a horse, to protect the gopīs. Spiritually, Keśī represents ego, which stands between us and Bhagavān. Ego arises when the self identifies with the body, yielding attachment to material desires and preventing us from realizing Kṛṣṇa, serving whom provides much greater fulfillment. Next, she narrates the killing of Cāṇūra and Muṣṭika, the two wrestlers at Kaṃsa's command. These represent kāma and krodha - desire and anger. These twins cause all issues and are the source of qualities obstructing us from realizing Bhagavān. The Gītā repeatedly instructs us to control these two. Desire is the seed of anger, as Bhagavān states in Gītā 2.62-63: "saṅgāt sañjāyate kāmaḥ kāmāt krodho 'bhijāyate krodhād bhavati sammohaḥ sammohāt smṛti-vibhramaḥ smṛti-bhraṃśād buddhi-nāśho buddhi-nāśhāt praṇaśhyati."
Such Kṛṣṇa is also called devādi deva. Śrīmad Bhāgavatam 7.8.37-39 beautifully describes how devas assembled to calm Nṛsiṃha: "Devas headed by Lord Brahmā, King Indra and Lord Śiva, including great saintly persons and residents of Pitṛloka, Siddhaloka, Vidyādhara-loka, and the planet of snakes. The Manus approached, as did chiefs of various planets. The angelic dancers, Gandharvas, Cāraṇas, Yakṣas, inhabitants of Kinnaraloka, Vetālas, inhabitants of Kimpuruṣa-loka, and personal servants of Viṣṇu like Sunanda and Kumuda. All approached the Lord, who glowed with intense light, offering individual obeisances and prayers." Hence, Yaśodā's darling Kṛṣṇa is truly the Supreme. When He sees devotees striving to reach Him, these surrendered souls, He feels their distress and surely comes to them, bringing fruits to their spiritual aspirations.
