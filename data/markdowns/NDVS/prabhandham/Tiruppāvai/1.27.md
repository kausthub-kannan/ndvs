## english_scripts

kūḍārai vellum śīr kōvindā !
un tannaippāḍi ppaṟai koṇḍu yām peṟu śammānam
nāḍu pugazum pariśināl nanṟāga
śūḍagamē tōḻ vaḻaiyē tōḍē śevi ppūvē
pāḍagamē enṟanaiya pal kalanum yām aṇivōm
āḍai uḍuppōm adan pinnē pāl śōṟu
mūḍa ney peydu muzaṅgai vazivāra
kūḍi irundu kuḻirndēlōr em pāvāy

## meaning

O Govinda! You, whose auspicious attributes are ever-abundant, are the destroyer of those who turn away from your grace.
What we seek, through our praise and by receiving the parai (divine drum), is not mere reward—but the glory of your acceptance, known and celebrated by all.

We wish to adorn ourselves for this moment of union—with bangles encircling our forearms, armlets gracing our upper arms, earrings for both the lower and upper lobes—some set with flowers, others with gems. Anklets shall chime on our feet, and over it all, we will drape ourselves in resplendent sarees.

Then, in joy and intimacy, we shall be seated beside you. Together, we will relish a sweet feast—pāyasam—warm rice pudding, enriched with milk, ghee, and sugar. Such is our delight in this divine meal that, as we eat with abandon, the rich sweetness will run down our arms, all the way to the elbows.

This, Govinda, is the bliss we anticipate—the reward of our vow: the joy of your company, the festivity of adornment, and the ecstasy of shared divine delight.

## purport

In this opening thought, Āṇḍāḷ celebrates the divine power of Govinda’s guṇas—His beauty, grace, and sweetness—which are so potent that they even conquer those who are antagonistic or uninterested in Him. Saints hesitate to approach due to their humility, lovers sulk in divine separation, ordinary souls remain indifferent, and enemies revile out of arrogance. Yet all are ultimately subdued—not by force, but by the sheer beauty of His compassion and excellence. His guṇas are the true warriors in this conquest, not weapons, and it is through them that even aversion turns into surrender.

As the gopikās continue their vrata, they humbly ask for boons—not for personal gain, but as divine sanction to serve and be adorned for their divine play. They request the “parai”—symbol of their vow—not merely as a ritual object, but as a token of His acceptance and grace. Their requests for ornaments, flowers, and silk garments symbolize their soul’s readiness to engage in līlā with the Lord, to immerse in His joy, and celebrate union through the playful, sacred music of devotion.

The detailed list of ornaments—bracelets, armlets, bangles, anklets, earrings, and garlands—are not mere finery, but spiritual metaphors. Each adornment reflects a quality bestowed by Him upon His devotees: protection, beauty, strength, and divine radiance. The flowers and garments symbolize inner purity and the sweetness of sāttvic expression. These are ornaments for the soul, not merely for the body, and represent the ātmā readying itself to play its part in the divine drama.

Their desire to sing, bathe, and rejoice in Govinda's company is not private indulgence but a celebration that unites them with all of creation. Their joy spills into rivers, trees, and cows—signs of a world harmonized by divine presence. The gopikās speak of bathing in sacred waters and singing Govinda’s glories in chorus, marking the merging of individual bhakti into collective ecstasy. The ritual becomes an offering of all nature in surrender to the Lord.

They envision sitting together with Kṛṣṇa, joyfully consuming a sweet dish—a metaphor for the bliss of divine communion. This food, simple yet rich, becomes sacred because it is shared with Him. Each grain represents grace, each spoonful divine presence. The imagery of laughter, milk flowing from mouths, and playful joy reflects the intimacy of the divine-child relationship—He is not a distant God but one who sits among devotees, laughs with them, eats with them, and becomes theirs.

Finally, the gopikās express a wish that their devotion be known throughout the world—not for pride, but so that others might be drawn to Him through their example. They yearn for the Lord's embrace not only privately but publicly, so that the world may witness the reward of true devotion. This echoes the āḻvār ideal: that one’s experience of divine love becomes the means for others' salvation. Their union with Govinda should not remain a secret—it should inspire the world to surrender.
