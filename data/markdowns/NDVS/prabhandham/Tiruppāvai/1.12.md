## english_scripts

nanaittillam śēṟākkum naṟcelvan taṅgāy
pani ttalai vīza nin vāśaṟ kaḍai paṭri

## meaning

Fondly thinking of their young calves and imagining they have stretched their mouths to their udders, herds of mahiṣīs (she-buffaloes) spontaneously rain milk through their teats. There are no gopālas at hand to milk them, yet the entire gośālā (cow shed) becomes drenched and miry with their flowing milk. "O sister of the wealthy owner of such magnificent herds! We stand here at your dvāra (door), holding the threshold, while the prabhāta's dew falls upon our heads. We are singing the glory of Śrī Rāma, the hṛdayeśa (charmer of hearts), who in righteous anger vanquished Rāvaṇa, the King of Laṅkā in the dakṣiṇa (south). Hearing our devotional singing, how is it that you do not deign to open your mukha (mouth)? Will you not take care of us? Why this deep nidrā (slumber)? The entire grāma (village) has come to know of your secret. Rise now from your sleep!

## purport

Āṇḍāḷ describes the young buffaloes whose udders overflow with milk as they think lovingly of their hungry calves. Though burdened by the pain of excess milk, their concern is not for themselves but for the calves separated from them. Their affection mirrors the compassion of Bhagavān, who cannot bear the suffering of the jīvātmas separated from Him. The milk begins to flow spontaneously, without the touch of the milkman or the calf itself, simply through remembrance born of love. Unlike clouds that release rain only at intervals, these buffaloes pour forth milk continuously, illustrating how true affection knows neither measure nor restraint. Āṇḍāḷ subtly asks the sleeping gopī why she remains unmoved by the distress of the devotees waiting outside when even animals express sorrow at separation.

The overflowing milk symbolizes the boundless grace of the ācāryas. Just as the buffaloes pour forth milk without being asked, great preceptors freely bestow the divine meanings of Bhagavān upon worthy seekers. Arjuna did not request the profound teachings of the Upaniṣads, yet Kṛṣṇa, out of compassion, revealed the wisdom of the *Śrīmad Bhagavad Gītā*. This truth is celebrated in the verse *sarvopaniṣado gāvo dogdhā gopālanandanaḥ*, where the Upaniṣads are compared to cows, Kṛṣṇa to the milkman, Arjuna to the calf, and the Gītā to the milk produced for the benefit of all devotees. Thus, if even the buffaloes of this household display such generosity, Āṇḍāḷ wonders why the gopī herself withholds the blessing of her company.

The abundance of milk drenches the entire courtyard, raising the question of why the milkman has neglected his task. The answer reveals his greatness. Like Lakṣmaṇa, who set aside even prescribed duties in order to accompany and serve Śrī Rāma and Sītā Devī in exile, this gopāla has become wholly immersed in service to Kṛṣṇa. Such abandonment of duty is not born of laziness or disregard for śāstra, but of complete dedication to Bhagavān. Therefore Āṇḍāḷ calls him *naṛ celvan*, one who possesses true wealth. Real wealth is not worldly prosperity but *Kṛṣṇa-kaiṅkarya*. Lakṣmaṇa, Gajendra, and Vibhīṣaṇa were all celebrated as truly wealthy because they placed service to the Lord above all else. Moreover, this wealth is contagious, inspiring devotion in all who come into contact with it.

Addressing the gopī as the sister of this blessed devotee, Āṇḍāḷ gently teases her. Her brother has abandoned the buffaloes under his care due to absorption in Kṛṣṇa-kaiṅkarya, while she has abandoned the devotees eagerly waiting at her doorstep. Yet this comparison ultimately glorifies her rather than criticizes her. Just as Vibhīṣaṇa dedicated himself to Śrī Rāma and his daughter Tṛjaṭā served Sītā Devī, the sister of such a devotee ought naturally to join the company of devotees undertaking the sacred vow. Standing amidst rain from above and streams of milk beneath their feet, the gopīs patiently wait outside her door, hoping she will emerge and join them.

When the gopī remains silent, the gopīs turn to singing the glories of Śrī Rāma. Though renowned for patience equal to that of Bhūdevī, Rāma became enraged whenever His devotees were harmed. Rāvaṇa had tormented the sages, abducted Sītā Devī, slain Jaṭāyu, opposed Vibhīṣaṇa, and insulted Hanumān. Therefore Rāma destroyed not merely Rāvaṇa himself but also his armies, weapons, power, and pride before finally ending his life. Unlike Kṛṣṇa, whose playful pranks often bewildered the women of Gokula, Rāma stands as the ideal protector who endured immense suffering for the sake of His consort and devotees. The remembrance of such a Lord naturally cools the hearts of those afflicted by separation.

For this reason Āṇḍāḷ calls Him *manattukkiniyān*, the One who delights the heart. The gopīs sing not of Kṛṣṇa's playful mischief but of Rāma's compassion and selfless protection. His divine name is capable of restoring life, hope, and comfort to those overwhelmed by sorrow. They urge the gopī to awaken and join their singing, not merely for their sake but for her own, since the remembrance of the Lord alone can relieve the pain of separation. Even Bhagavān rushed instantly to rescue Gajendra when called upon, yet this gopī remains asleep despite repeated appeals.

Finally, Āṇḍāḷ marvels at the depth of her slumber. The entire village now knows that the gopīs stand outside her house pleading for her to awaken. Her greatness is known to all, as is the devotion and helplessness of those waiting for her. Nothing remains hidden. Having praised her virtues, her family, her devotion, and the compassion of both Kṛṣṇa and Rāma, the gopīs make one final appeal: she should arise, join the assembly of devotees, and participate in the collective experience of praising the Lord, for such shared devotion is far superior to remaining alone, however exalted one's spiritual state may be.
