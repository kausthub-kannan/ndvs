## meaning

Like the lion who, after resting motionless in his mountain cave through the long monsoon, suddenly awakens—his eyes fierce and scanning the forest, his mane bristling, his limbs stretching wide—he shakes his mighty frame, roars aloud, and strides out, majestic and unstoppable— So too, O Lord, whose radiant form mirrors the deep blue hue of the ātasi flower, do arise from your divine repose. Step forth from your sacred abode, where time itself seems to pause in reverence, and ascend your peerless throne, the seat graced by endless celestial associations. Let your movements stir the very core of creation. Let your eyes fall upon us as the lion surveys his domain—not with wrath, but with the command of a guardian awakened. And then, we pray—listen. Lend your ear to our lowly yet earnest voices. We, who have waited, who have yearned, stand gathered at your gate, hoping that the King of kings might hear our call.

## purport

The lion, that has long lain asleep in his mountain-den during the rainy season, slowly begins to emerge. This monsoon season—chāturmāsya kāla—is not merely a meteorological period, but a sacred juncture for kings, poets, lovers, and ascetics. During these four months, wandering mendicants (yatis) stay in a fixed place and engage in deep meditation, undertaking the vow of chāturmāsya dīkṣā. Lovers separated by distance are tormented by longing, while poets, stirred by the cloud-filled skies, give birth to inspired verse. Kings suspend martial activities as roads vanish beneath rainwaters, and even enmities seem to dissolve.

It is said that during this time, Śrīmannārāyaṇa enters yogic slumber in His heavenly abode, Vaikuṇṭha. Lions, abandoning the hunt, retreat to their dens with their mates to rest. Śrī Rāma, though anguished by separation from Sītā, assists Sugrīva by slaying Vāli, only to spend the monsoon in the Malyavat mountains alongside Lakṣmaṇa, waiting for the season to pass. Sugrīva, reunited with his kingdom and his wife, loses himself in pleasure, forgetting his debt to Rāma. Rāma, beholding the rainclouds, is overcome with grief, thinking of His distant beloved.

The raincloud, symbolizing both hope and melancholy, has captivated sages like Vālmīki, Kālidāsa, and Śūdraka. In Kālidāsa’s Meghadūta, the cloud is animated as a loving messenger. This season signifies the transition from summer to winter, bringing inner and outer fertility—nourishing harvests and awakening the milk of cows. But alongside this physical rain lies another rain—an inner, spiritual shower. Śrī Yāmunācārya describes this as follows: “aviveka-ghaṇāndha digmukhe, bahudhā santata-duḥkha-darśini,
Bhagavan bhava-durdine pathitaṁ, mām avalokaya acyuta!” Ignorance, the thick cloud of aviveka, blinds the soul, causing it to drift in all directions, unable to discern its path. This ignorance rains down endless sorrow upon the jīva, binding it in saṁsāra. The gopikās, feeling like wanderers lost in torrential rain, plead for the Lord’s compassion to show them the path of liberation.

But there is another, redemptive rain: the downpour of divine grace (anugraha), rising from the waves of the ocean of His mercy, blown by the wind of compassion. This divine shower alone can save us. As sentient beings are tossed about on the sea of saṁsāra, it is God, out of sheer mercy, who rescues them. In both creation and dissolution, His grace operates—drawing the universe into Himself, filling all space, and entering into deep yogic sleep, like the lion in the mountain-cave. During pralaya, all beings—great or low—merge into Him. He shines silently in the cavern of the Vedas, veiled from manifest perception. In that undivided state, there is nothing distinct from Him. As the Upaniṣads declare: “sa devah asīd ekam eva advitīyam” – That Deva alone was, One without a second.

Just as the lion lies unseen in his den, the Supreme rests in a state far beyond comprehension. Yet, this state is not inert; it is alive with potential, with all of His auspicious attributes latent within. Dharma, the Supreme itself, is the means of abhyudaya (temporal progress) and niḥśreyasa (eternal beatitude). The Vedas declare that the essence of Dharma lies hidden in a cave: “dharmasya tattvaṁ nihitaṁ guhāyām.” This truth is subtle, elusive, and perceptible only through intense effort. As Sage Bharadvāja once saw the Vedas as towering mountains, so too is the Supreme veiled—its glory only revealed through persistent seeking.
