## meaning

O Lord, deeply fond of your devotees and radiant like a flawless nīla-maṇi! You, who made the impossible possible by resting within a tender fig leaf—compressed, yet complete—we have come to you, intent on performing the Mārgaśīrṣa bath, as done by our noble ancestors and learned sages. If you would graciously listen, we shall present our requests: grant us śaṅkhas white as milk, echoing like your Pañcajanya, and paṣai drums—grand and thunderous—along with groups of singers devoted to your praise.

## purport

The gopikās, consumed by their own love, begin to realize that Kṛṣṇa’s affection for them is even more intense—deeper and more boundless than their own. Unlike their wavering emotions, his is vast like an ocean. So great is his fondness that it leaves him paralyzed in their absence—unable to even act to reunite with them. They, however, manage to initiate the vratam, taking effort to reach him, while he remains frozen in longing. As they awaken him, they discover not the distant, absolute God they earlier praised as Nārāyaṇa or Devadeva, but one whose true nature is defined by overwhelming compassion for his devotees. He is malē—the embodiment of affectionate fondness.

Across the epics, the Lord’s defining quality is love for his devotees. Śrī Rāma protected even Vibhīṣaṇa, Ravana’s brother, not out of duty but from joy. When Hanumān tells him of Sītā’s state, Rāma mourns that he cannot live even a moment without her. Similarly, Kṛṣṇa, out of love for the Pāṇḍavas, takes up roles unbecoming of a Supreme Being—a charioteer, a messenger—just to serve them. In truth, the Lord’s efforts to reach the Āḻvārs and his devotees surpass even their efforts to reach him.

Even his birth and upbringing among the cowherds is not merely divine leelā; it’s a secret mission, not just to escape Kaṁsa, but to avoid separation from the gopikās. He appears as one maddened with love, pulling them into the same divine madness. They see the signs of this madness in his very form—his look, his stillness, his helpless gaze. When questioned, “How do you know I love you?” they reply, “Your form does not lie—it reveals the truth within.” They address him as Manivanna—one whose complexion shines like a blue sapphire. Just as an object placed in a jewel box reflects through the jewel’s surface, his inner fondness radiates outward through his form. Even Śrī Lakṣmī is captivated, making his chest her eternal home. His form is so beautiful that people love him even if he rejects or torments them. Like a rare gem, his very nature and luster make him invaluable, precious, and worthy of devotion.

A gem is cherished by its owner, bringing life to them and prestige in society. Likewise, those who possess Kṛṣṇa are deemed alive, and the world respects them. The gem's worth is known only to those who understand its value—so too, only those who realize the true nature of God recognize his infinite worth. He, like the gem, is for others, not for himself. The one who has him desires nothing else. Hence, the gopikās fittingly address him as Manivanna. When asked about their visit, the gopikās say they seek ritual materials for the Mārgazhi bath. While its surface intention is for public welfare (rains), its deeper intent is communion with Kṛṣṇa. Though they have already surrendered to him, they still undertake this ritual—so that others, observing them, do not abandon faith or scriptural practices. Their worship becomes not obligation but kainkaryam—consecrated service.

Kṛṣṇa questions: Is this rite prescribed by the Vedas? The gopikās reply—it is rooted in ancestral tradition (ācāra), which the Dharmaśāstras uphold as greater than scriptural injunctions. The words and practices of those firmly established in dharma are the highest authority. Even Kṛṣṇa, in the Gītā, affirms that the world follows the great man’s conduct—so their practice stands as pramāṇa. The gopikās emphasize that they aren’t versed in ritualistic Vedic practices like animal sacrifice. They are cowherds, grounded in lived tradition. Their ancestors revered Kṛṣṇa as Nārāyaṇa and passed on the conviction that only he can grant the parai (drum of fulfillment). Hence, they seek not just mantras, but the dwaya-mantra—passed down and sanctioned by practice. Scripture confirms what tradition already lives.

They now offer to share their list—if Kṛṣṇa would listen. But he, mesmerized by their beauty, is lost in admiration. Noticing this, they gently rebuke him—“You are feasting on us with your eyes while we are still famished, waiting to feast on your form, words, and presence.” They seek mutual enjoyment, not one-sided. They urge him to awaken from this trance and truly listen to their needs. The first item they request is a conch—white as milk, thunderous in sound, and resembling Kṛṣṇa’s own Pāñcajanya. This conch, which once struck fear in Kurukṣetra and joy in Rukmiṇī’s heart, must now awaken the spiritual earth—the prakṛti—to release them from bondage. The conch symbolizes praṇava (Oṁ), which awakens the knowledge that the jīva belongs solely to God, cutting ties with nature and ego.

Next, they ask for large drums (parai), hollow and loud—symbolizing the word namah (“not mine”). It teaches the jīva that all action is God’s, and that the self is merely his instrument. This humility—rejection of doership—is essential to liberation. The drum, worn on the chest, echoes the centrality of “namah” in the tirumaṅtra. They request devotees to sing benedictions (pallāṇḍu) as they proceed—saints who lovingly fear for the devotees’ well-being. More than God himself, the gopikās seek the company of such saints. Fellowship with devotees not only protects but ensures union with Kṛṣṇa. The lamp they ask for represents the knowledge that they belong to such saints. Just as light reveals and guides, this conviction reveals their true path—one of belonging, not autonomy. Even Lakṣmī is this light—she shows that it is through her grace one reaches Nārāyaṇa. They request a flag and canopy. The flag—visible from afar—symbolizes consecrated service that announces one’s allegiance to the Lord. The awning protects from the dew of bhoktṛtva buddhi—the egoistic sense of enjoying service rather than offering it selflessly. This dew, if unchecked, tarnishes the soul’s natural ornament—service born of surrender.

Through their requests, the gopikās have asked for: (1) knowledge of belonging to God (śeṣatvam), (2) dependence on Him (pāratantryam), (3) company of devotees, (4) conviction of belonging to saints, (5) consecrated service, and (6) elimination of egoistic enjoyment. These are the six limbs of prapatti—the complete surrender that alone brings God. Finally, they invoke his cosmic form—the infant on the fig-leaf floating during the Pralaya. If that Kṛṣṇa, the womb of the universe, could lie upon a leaf with all worlds in his belly, surely granting these ritual materials is no feat. His omnipotence (aiśvarya) and intimacy (sauśīlya) converge in the accessible form before them. All that is needed is his mercy (aruḷ). With this last appeal, they seal their surrender.
