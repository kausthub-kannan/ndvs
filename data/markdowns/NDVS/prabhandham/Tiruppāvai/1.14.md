## english_scripts

śeṅgazunīr vāy negizndāmbal vāy kūmbina kāṇ
śeṅgal poḍi kkūrai veṇbaṟ tavattavar

## meaning

O perfect one in worship! The red lotuses in the well situated in the garden to the rear of the house are blooming; and the black tulips are folding up. Arise! The ascetics who have fine sets of white teeth and who are filled with a spirit of renunciation have put on their ochre robes and are going to their temples for worship. Arise! You have pledged your word that you will yourself come and wake us up. Have you forgotten it, O shameless girl! Get up! O adroit talker! Come out to sing the glory of the Lord of the lotus-eyes and long arms, who holds the śaṅkha and the cakra in His hands.

## purport

Trying to wake up the gopī, Āṇḍāl states that the tulips have shrunk, and lotuses are blooming. Tulips are nocturnal plants, and lotuses bloom during the day. This symbolizes that the night has broken. But the gopī is not ready to accept this. She argues, saying, "You are seeing my eyes, which are slowly opening up, and in happiness, you call them lotuses, but the dawn has not broken." Āṇḍāl continues to emphasize the blossoming of the lotus by saying they have bloomed in "your garden." The purpose of using "your" is to give a swift shock to the gopī. Being a mumukṣu, the gopī has given up mamakāra and ahaṅkāra—the sense of "mine" and "I." She knows everything, including herself, belongs to Kṛṣṇa alone. But when Āṇḍāl says "your," she fears mamakāra. Mṛtyu, i.e., death, which leads to rebirth, truly occurs as long as this jīvātma holds the egotistic sense of "mine," as expressed in Dvyākṣarastu bhaven mṛtyuḥ, i.e., the death in the two-letter word "mama." Eternal life or liberation is in the three-letter word "na mama." One who realizes that he is not the bhoktā (enjoyer) but Bhagavān is, and that he is not the kartā (doer) but Bhagavān is, becomes merely an agency for the Supreme to act. Such a person has liberated himself from the results of his actions. Karma yogis who reach this state finally surrender to the Supreme as they realize that they themselves are His and are meant to be enjoyed by the Supreme. One who attains such a stage finds his lotus blooming under the sunlight of knowledge of Bhagavān. The lotus here refers to the blossoming of the suṣumṇā-nāḍī. From here, the jīvātma travels via the Arcirādi mārga, attaining Vaikuṇṭha—the place without return—where the muktātma enjoys bliss equal to that of Īśvara, who is omnipresent. The shrinking of the tulip represents the removal of the knowledge of self-independence. We are all asvatantra (dependent) and depend entirely on Īśvara. Realizing this, the lotus blooms.

The gopī argues, saying, "The lotuses are just blooming, and the tulip is just shrinking. It is still too early for the sun to rise." Āṇḍāl provides another justification, stating that ascetics dressed in red robes have gathered here, walking towards the temple, their teeth shining white. They wear the sacred mṛttikā (white clay) and have śrīchūrṇa on their foreheads. She says, "Wake up! Dawn has arrived!" The ascetics have broken the ochre stone to form the mṛttikā. The ochre stone represents the manas (mind), which distracts us, and red represents attachment. By grinding and wearing it, the ascetics have controlled their manas to show love only to Bhagavān. They are ācāryas who carry the kuñcikola to open the temple doors. This kuñcikola represents the upadeśa-mudrā. The upadeśa-mudrā, where the forefinger touches the thumb and the three fingers stand apart, symbolizes that the forefinger and thumb represent jīvātma and Īśvara, respectively, while the three fingers represent the three guṇas—sattva, rajas, and tamas. Renouncing these guṇas and adopting śuddha-sattva, the jīvātma attains sāyujya with Bhagavān.

The gopī still argues, saying, "Ascetics always sing and serve the Lord at all times. How does this represent the arrival of dawn?" Āṇḍāl now says, "You promised to wake us up, and now we are waking you!" It must be understood that Āṇḍāl asks the gopī to wake up and attain complete fullness. The gopī promised to wake up, i.e., to see Bhagavān beyond Māyā. Now, she must help others too, like the ācāryas. It is only through the association of other devotees that one can attain fullness. Āṇḍāl scolds her lovingly, saying, "You are of divine knowledge. O shameless girl! O adroit talker! Get up. Are you feeling ashamed that you could not come early and wake us up? No, do not feel that way. By your constant association with Śrī Rāma, you may have developed a feeling of bashful embarrassment. When the ṛṣis came to Śrī Rāma in Daṇḍakāraṇya and sought His protection, Śrī Rāma felt embarrassed and apologized to them: 'Alas, I should have come to you venerable ones myself and given you protection. Instead, I have unwittingly allowed time to slip until you came to me and told me of your troubles. I feel deeply ashamed of my lapse.'" Similarly, Āṇḍāl suggests that the gopī might feel ashamed for making others come to her. However, she reminds her that it is her nature to help those who depend on her. 

Āṇḍāl then asks her to sing about the lotus-eyed Lord, the holder of the conch and discus. The divine implements—the conch and discus—serve distinct spiritual purposes. The conch represents the praṇava (Oṃkāra) and serves to summon both straying cows and wandering souls back to the divine fold. The gopikas particularly envied the conch's fortune of constant contact with Kṛṣṇa. The discus, known as Sudarśana, helps devotees achieve a clear vision of the Supreme Self and acts independently to execute Kṛṣṇa's will, protecting devotees while keeping enemies at bay. Both implements work in harmony to protect supplicants and instill fear in enemies. Kṛṣṇa's lotus eyes hold special significance in this divine imagery, displaying various aspects, from humility to authority, depending on the context. To the gopikas, these eyes could appear meek, clever, authoritative, or charming, while also serving to protect and enthrall the residents of the Heavenly Abode.
