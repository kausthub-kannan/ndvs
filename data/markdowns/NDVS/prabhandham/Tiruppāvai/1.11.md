## meaning

O golden creeper, sprouted in the community of blameless gopālas, who excel in milking herds of cows (which, though having mothered calves, are still young themselves) and who possess great strength and valor in facing and fighting enemies! O maiden, with hips resembling the hood of a serpent in its hole! O girl with beautiful tresses shining like the peacock in the forest! Come! Your kith and kin and companions have all arrived. They are assembled in the courtyard of your house. They are glorifying the name of Śrī Kṛṣṇa, whose body shines with the hue of the dark cloud. How is it that you still remain dull and mute? O wealthy maiden! Explain to us the meaning of your sleep.

## purport

Āṇḍāḷ describes how the gopālas are milking the cows, which are countless in number. The clan of cowherds possessed cows in the lakhs (hundreds of thousands). Indeed, Āṇḍāḷ emphasizes their vast numbers, drawing a parallel to how the number of births we jīvātmas have taken, the attributes of Bhagavān, and the incarnations He has taken are all beyond counting. These divine cows maintain their beauty and youthfulness, yielding abundant milk due to their association with Kṛṣṇa, His graceful touch, and the divine melody of His flute. They remain eternally twenty-five years old due to Bhagavān's constant presence.

These cows symbolize the mukta jīvātmas (liberated souls). Having been freed from the cycle of saṃsāra and devoid of all karma and vāsanās, they remain in eternal association with the Supreme Being in Vaikuṇṭha. The presence of Bhagavān invariably brings ānanda (bliss and happiness). This is illustrated in the Rāmāyaṇa when Daśaratha cried in anguish as Rāma departed for fourteen years of exile – it was as if his very happiness was being sent away. The significance of twenty-five years holds deep meaning. In the Rāmāyaṇa, when Sītā Devī converses with Anasūyā about dharma, she mentions her age as eighteen while her husband Rāma is twenty-five. The world comprises twenty-four tattvas of prakṛti, with the twenty-fifth tattva being the jīvātma. When a jīvātma correctly identifies itself and doesn't conflate itself with prakṛti tattva, it gains clarity about all tattvas and its own nature. This enables it to recognize itself as the śarīra (body) of Īśvara, that is, Kṛṣṇa.

The gopālas milk these numerous divine cows, a task that distinguishes them from other gopālas of different regions. This is analogous to a brāhmaṇa who has mastered the Vedas and their auxiliary texts, earning respect beyond their prescribed duties. These gopālas represent ācāryas. They perform their duties knowing well that it is not the duties themselves but Kṛṣṇa alone who grants liberation. Yet, they perform their duties out of pure love for Kṛṣṇa, finding satisfaction in His happiness. Just as the gopālas protect the cows from enemies, the ācāryas defeat internal enemies like desire (kāma) and anger (krodha) that obstruct their daily spiritual practices. The gopālas and gopikās possess courage similar to the Lord's and show great mercy to those who repent their mistakes.

Continuing to praise her attributes, Āṇḍāḷ offers a beautiful comparison, describing the gopī as a golden creeper. Creepers grow dependent on support, lest they fall and break. Similarly, we jīvātmas are not independent – our dependence is on Bhagavān, who alone is independent. Whether we realize this truth or not, it remains unchangeable. When we do realize our dependence on Īśvara, as the gopī has, such jīvātmas become mumukṣus or liberated souls. They shine like gold, hence Āṇḍāḷ's metaphor of the golden creeper.

Āṇḍāḷ further draws a unique comparison of the gopī, likening her waist to a coiled snake in a hole, followed by the image of a peacock unfurling its feathers. This progression from the coiled snake to the spreading peacock feathers represents the evolution of devotion. Referencing Rāmānujācārya's Śaraṇāgati Gadyam, where he prays to Mahālakṣmī for blessings of devotion: "parabhakti parājñāna paramabhaktikṛta." Beautifully, the same Mahālakṣmī, here as Āṇḍāḷ, explains these stages of devotion in a prapanna. Parabhakti is the stage where separation from Bhagavān becomes unbearable, inducing viraha. Parajñāna leads to seeing Him everywhere, and finally, Paramabhakti is the state where serving Bhagavān becomes the devotee's sole desire.\
Āṇḍāḷ continues by describing her immense wealth – the greatest wealth of kaiṅkarya (service to the Lord). In the Rāmāyaṇa, none was as wealthy as Lakṣmaṇa, who possessed kaiṅkarya lakṣmī, enabling him to serve the Lord eternally. Āṇḍāḷ beseeches the gopī to share this kaiṅkarya experience with them, asking her to awaken from sleep. This presents the contrast between kaivalya and mokṣa. Kaivalya is a state where the jīvātma immerses itself in self-bliss. Though this appears like liberation, it remains incomplete and imperfect. True mokṣa is attained only when the jīvātma realizes its nature as a servant of the Lord and completely surrenders. Similarly, sharing spiritual experiences with fellow devotees surpasses solitary practice. Hence, Āṇḍāḷ calls upon the gopī to awaken and praise the dark-complexioned Kṛṣṇa.
