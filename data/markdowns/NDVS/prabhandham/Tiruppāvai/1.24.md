## meaning

Blessed be Thy divine feet, O Vāmana, with which Thou didst measure the three worlds—received as alms from King Mahābali, who, though possessing what was not his, caused distress to the amṛtas. Blessed be Thy strong and mighty arms, O Rāghava, with which Thou didst shatter the walls of Laṅkā, burn its splendid mansions, and destroy Rāvaṇa and his army for abducting Sītā Devī. Blessed be Thy wondrous glory, O Kṛṣṇa, when as a child, with an effortless stretch of Thy foot, Thou didst crush the demon lurking in the cart, which turned from protector to threat. Blessed be Thy sturdy legs, planted firm—one forward, one behind—when Thou hurled the demon-possessed calf upon the wood-apple tree, felling both in a single act. Blessed be Thy compassion, revealed in lifting Govardhana as a great umbrella to shield the gopas and cows from the thunderous storm Indra unleashed in his anger at being denied worship. Blessed be Thy divine discus, the Vēlāyudha, which in Thy hands whirls as the remover of obstacles and the vanquisher of foes. We have come to celebrate these valorous deeds and receive from Thee the parai, the sacred drum of our nōnpu. May Thou, O Lord, accept our devotion and grant our prayer.

## purport

This pāsuram opens with aṉṉiṟu—“then”—pointing to the time when Śrī Vāmana, the dwarf-incarnation, stretched his foot across the universe, humbling the pride of Mahābali. It concludes with iṉṟu—“now”—referring to the present moment when the gopikās lovingly impose strain upon the divine feet of Kaṇṇaṉ (Kṛṣṇa) by their service. These two instances—then and now—are equal in intimacy and reverence for the devotees. In three key pāsurams (3, 17, and 24), Āṇḍāḷ glorifies the Trivikrama avatāra, revealing its intimate relation to the accessible and enchanting avatāra of Kṛṣṇa. Vāmana’s act of laying claim to the universe without distinction, irrespective of people's desire or worthiness, mirrors the irresistible charm of Kṛṣṇa that pulls the gopikās to Him, shedding social norms, familial restraint, and personal pride (naṉṉeñjam, feminine propriety). The same divine feet that crushed the ego of Mahābali now trample upon the vanity of the gopikās' womanly pride.

Mahābali’s fall represents the human soul's (jīvātmā’s) egoistic bondage, forged through identification with prakṛti (primordial nature). He imagined himself the lord of the worlds, mistaking his transient ownership and actions as independent of Īśvara’s will. By placing His divine foot upon Bali, the Lord demonstrated that all possessions, power, and very selfhood (ahaṅkāra) are to be surrendered at His feet. In that act of subjugation, there were no contemporaries to celebrate or sing pōṟṟi (praise) for those divine feet. Now, as the gopikās see themselves freed from the grip of bodily pride and feminine hesitation by surrendering to Kṛṣṇa’s feet, they recall Trivikrama’s act and redeem that forgotten moment of glory by singing benediction. Pōṟṟi kaḷaḷaṭikaḷ!—Blessed are those tender yet all-encompassing feet!

Āṇḍāḷ, the daughter of Periyāḻvār, inherits his bhakti tradition of pallāṇḍu—auspicious benedictions. When Periyāḻvār was granted vision of the Lord during his royal court debate, he instinctively began to sing Pallāṇḍu pallāṇḍu! fearing an evil eye may fall upon the Lord. So too, Āṇḍāḷ begins this verse with pōṟṟi pōṟṟi—singing the auspiciousness of Kṛṣṇa’s limbs and deeds, especially His feet. She poetically feels that even when Vāmana sanctified the cosmos with His divine touch, no one was there to celebrate that act. Thus, the gopikās take it upon themselves to offer that missing maṅgalaśāsanam. Vāmana’s soft, lotus-like feet, so delicate that even Śrī and Bhū Devīs feared touching them lest they become sore, trod upon the rough, stone-laden earth with grace and compassion. Yet the very earth, dull and ungrateful, failed to rejoice or even recognize the sacred contact. Still, He claimed it all in silent, sovereign love.

This indifference did not bother Trivikrama; He stood above all, unshaken. Bali sulked, having lost his kingdom. Śukrācārya tried in vain to stop the offering. The devas, reclaiming their realms, departed without offering gratitude. No one offered even a simple pōṟṟi to those aching, divine feet. The gopikās, feeling anguished by this oversight, now amend it. Kaḷaḷ pōṟṟi!—let those divine feet be praised! Through this, Āṇḍāḷ not only praises the act but the Lord’s unfathomable saulabhya (accessibility), His readiness to endure for the sake of reclaiming the jīvātmā. His act of measuring the worlds was not just conquest—it was a reclamation, a gesture of affection, even when unrecognized. And yet, He did not pause for thanks; instead, He carried out the act for dharma, for re-establishing rightful order. The gopikās, unlike the others, understand this and sing with full heart.

In Trivikrama’s feet, the gopikās see the essence of śaraṇāgati—surrender. For them, it is not a historical act but a present, living experience. The pride that Mahābali bore is not unlike the pride they see subdued within themselves. The yearning they carry is not simply to praise, but to prevent harm—to guard those limbs from any evil, any fatigue, any oversight. And so they sing, not once, but again and again, in a mad repetition born out of love, fear, and devotion. They internalize pōṟṟi as not a formality but a sacred, essential act—protective, possessive, and devotional. It is their natural tongue, their nāma, their lifeline. As Periyāḻvār could not stop with a single pallāṇḍu, Āṇḍāḷ too cannot refrain from her cascade of pōṟṟi.

This is not literary; this is lived bhakti. The gopikās’ glorification of Trivikrama's feet is not just remembrance—it is re-experiencing. It is an act of love where each pōṟṟi carries within it a shield, a flower, a tear, a touch. Their glorification stems not from witnessing, but from identification. They do not view the Lord's actions from afar but step into it—feeling His pain, carrying His strain, and rejoicing in His compassion. They offer benediction not as spectators, but as intimate ātmābandhus—souls entwined with the Divine. And thus, they begin the hymn with what was once left unsaid: Pōṟṟi kaḷaḷaṭikaḷ—blessed be those divine feet that once walked the worlds, and now walk into our hearts.
