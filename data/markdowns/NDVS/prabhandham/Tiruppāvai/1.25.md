## english_scripts

orutti maganāy ppiṟandu
ōr iravil orutti maganāy oḻittu vaḻara
tarikkilān āgi ttān tīṅgu ninainda
karuttai ppizaippittu kkañjan vayiṭril
neruppenna ninṟa neḍumālē
unnai aruttittu vandōm paṟai tarudi yāgil
tiruttakka śelvamum śēvagamum yām pāḍi
varuttamum tīrndu magizndēlōr em pāvāy

## meaning

O Kṛṣṇa! To fulfil the wish of peerless Devakī-devī to bear Bhagavān as her son, you were born to her, bearing the conch (śaṅkha), discus (cakra), mace (gadā), and lotus (padma) in your four arms. Yet, that very night, you became the son of glorious Yaśodā, who alone was blessed to bind, chastise, and delight in your divine līlās.

Growing up in secret at Vṛndāvana, you thwarted every wicked plot of Kaṁsa and destroyed the one who sought to destroy you. Ever compassionate to those who seek refuge, we too come to you, desiring nothing but your grace. If pleased, grant us the parai.

Our weariness melts into bliss when we sing of your unmatched wealth—coveted even by Lakṣmī—and your countless heroic deeds.

## purport

Kṛṣṇa, though born to Dēvaki, the illustrious queen of Mathurā, did not remain even for a full night with her. That same night, under the weight of divine ordainment, he was transferred to Gokula to become the foster-son of Yaśōdā, the cowherdess of unparalleled fortune. The Āḻvārs and ācāryas refer to both these exalted women as orutti—"a woman"—not out of disregard, but out of deep reverence. According to traditional etiquette, one avoids naming the most sacred persons directly, choosing instead such respectful circumlocutions. Dēvaki was the womb that bore the Para Brahman—ēkam ēva advitīyam—without undergoing any change herself, making her utterly unique among all mothers in the cosmos. She bore in her womb not a jīva with karma, but the Kāraṇa bhūta—the Supreme Cause of all creation.

Yet, it was Yaśōdā who received the full sweetness (mādhurya) of the Lord’s vātśalya-līlā, his childhood pastimes. While Dēvaki had the privilege of giving birth to the Supreme Lord in his divya-mūrti, it was Yaśōdā who got to nurse, scold, cajole, and even bind him. She played with him, bathed him, dressed him, and watched him grow—privileges denied to even the devas. Though Kṛṣṇa was born of Dēvaki, he never cried in her arms nor drank her milk. He first expressed his human behavior—crying, suckling—only after reaching Yaśōdā. Hence, even Kṛṣṇa himself regarded Yaśōdā as his true mother and addressed Nanda as his father, stammering the words “adē appā” when asked by villagers.

His descent was marked by utmost secrecy. He who is beyond time and space entered time during that unique, transcendent night—a night like no other—when Yamunā parted, chains broke, and Ādiśeṣa bore him across to safety. That divine night, oriravu, is described as “without a second” just like Dēvaki and Yaśōdā are “without equals.” The Supreme descended, bearing the symbols of his transcendence—śaṅkha, cakra, gadā, and padma—and yet, subjected himself to the distress of secrecy and exile, for the sake of his devotees. The joy of birth, the celebration due to Dēvaki and Vasudēva, was denied. There was no grand cradle ceremony, no open celebration—just silence and hiddenness. For the Lord’s avatāra is not merely a spectacle—it is an act of anugraha (grace) and anubhava (intimacy).

Kaṁsa, the embodiment of ahaṅkāra (ego), could not bear the very thought (ninaindu) of the Lord. Though he never saw Kṛṣṇa directly, he was agitated upon hearing of him through Nārada, just as the ego trembles at the truth of the Self. This isn’t merely mythic villainy—it reflects the internal struggle of the bound jīva, which resists the divine within. Kaṁsa devised numerous cruel plans to thwart destiny, sending asuras like Pūtanā, Śakaṭāsura, and Tṛṇāvarta—all reflections of various inner obstructions to bhakti. But Kṛṣṇa, out of compassion for his devotees, consumed that very fire of fear and redirected it into Kaṁsa’s heart. What once tormented Yaśōdā and the gopikās was turned into Kaṁsa’s own internal agony. The Lord, like Anjanēya who used Sītā’s grief to burn Laṅkā, transformed the anxiety of his devotees into Kaṁsa’s demise.

The gopikās, aware of this entire sequence of divine acts, say “unnai aruṭṭittu vāndōm”—“We have come here desiring only you.” Not your miracles, not your protection, not even parai (the ritual drum). Their bhakti is ananya—exclusive, unswerving. They seek not reward, nor relief, nor ritualistic outcomes—but the Svarūpa (essential being) of the Lord himself. Their act of coming—through night and dew, forsaking modesty and societal censure—demonstrates that their prīti (affection) surpasses even his dayā (compassion). If he has descended from Vaikuṇṭha for their sake, they too have left behind every comfort and identity just to be with him. Their bhakti is not transactional—it is pūrṇa, complete.

When questioned about the parai, they gently clarify: “We will receive it only if it is your will. We do not ask—your will alone matters.” This reflects the viśuddha-sattva nature of true devotion. Even if Kṛṣṇa offers something they hadn’t sought, they will accept—not out of desire, but obedience and surrender. Asking would imply agency; receiving with humility reflects the śaraṇāgati tattva.

And in the final lines, they assert: “tiruttakka selvamum, śrīvagamum, yam paṭi varuttamum, tīrndu magiḻndu”—“We are relieved of every strain and filled with bliss by singing your glory and magnificence, which even Lakṣmī herself desires.” His aiśvarya (opulence) and vīrya (valour) are such that even Śrī Lakṣmī, the goddess of wealth and prosperity, seeks shelter in him. Kṛṣṇa is not merely Śrī-nātha (consort of Lakṣmī)—he is Śriyah Śrīḥ—the wealth of Lakṣmī herself. His bhāgyam (fortune) is self-existent, inexhaustible, and ever-fresh. Singing about such a Lord removes not just fatigue, but even the trace of grief (duḥkha). Just as nāmasankīrtana (Pundarīkākṣa-nāma-saṅkīrtana) is the sustenance (pāṭhēya) for the spiritual journey, their song has become their bliss.
