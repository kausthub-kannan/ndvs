## english_scripts

uḻḻattē uṟaiyum mālai
uḻḻuvān uṇarvonṟillā
kaḻḻattēn nānum toṇḍāy
toṇḍukkē kōlam pūṇḍu
uḻḻuvār uḻḻiṭrellām
uḍan irundaṟidi enṟu
veḻgippōy ennuḻḻē nān
vilavaṟa ccirittiṭṭēnē !

## meaning

Oh Lord Ranganatha! You are dwelling in the heart of everyone and affectionate towards all creatures. I do not have adequate knowledge even to contemp late upon You. However, I am a deceitful person. I pose my- self as your servant and so, put on the guise of doing service under You; when I am able to  recognize that You are the in-dweller, and so you are capable of gleaning the thought (idea) of every- one, being ever a companion - I feel quite ashamed and even then, I abandon You (i.e. move off from your holy shrine), and so laugh at myself as my ribs could break into pieces within my heart where, indeed, You hid yourself.

## purport

The Āḻvār contemplates the omnipresence of Emperumān, particularly His dwelling in the heart (uṟaiyum), the springboard of thoughts. He emphasizes the impossibility of deceiving the Lord, who is described as Mālai (the supreme one), knowing and controlling everything. The Āḻvār laments his lack of spiritual knowledge: "uḷḷuvān uṇarvu illā" (without the knowledge to meditate on His indwelling presence). He confesses to having no understanding of either the jīvātmā or Paramātmā, nor of the body's transient nature.
Calling himself "kaḷḷattēṉ" (a thief), the Āḻvār admits to deceiving both the foolish and the learned by feigning ignorance or humility. He acknowledges the impropriety of his attempts at service (toṇḍu), citing Śrī Rāmāyaṇa to illustrate the despicable nature of one unseen by Rāma.

Āḻvār confesses to wearing the external symbols of devotion (Ūrdhva puṇḍras, divine weapons) and reciting divine names without true belief, misleading others about his spiritual status. The realization dawns: "uḷḷuvār uḷḷiṟṟellām uḍaṉ irundu aṟidi eṉṟu" (You know all thoughts of those who think). The Āḻvār references Śakuntalā's words to Duṣyanta from the Mahābhārata, highlighting the futility of deceit before the omniscient Lord.

Overcome with shame (veḷgip pōy), the Āḻvār attempts to flee the Lord's presence, only to realize the impossibility of finding a place devoid of His presence. This realization leads to self-deprecating laughter (eṉṉuḷḷē nāṉ viḷavaṟac cirittiṭṭēṉē), so intense that his ribs metaphorically break. The passage concludes by comparing the Āḻvār's attempt to leave Emperumān's presence to one adorning oneself before committing suicide, highlighting the absurdity of trying to escape the omnipresent divine. This poignant self-realization is juxtaposed with Arjuna's moment of despair in the Bhagavad Gītā, emphasizing the Āḻvār's spiritual crisis and the profound understanding of the inescapable nature of the divine presence.
