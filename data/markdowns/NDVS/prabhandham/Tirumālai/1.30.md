## english_scripts

manattil ōr tūymai illai
vāyil ōr in śol illai
śinattināl śeṭram nōkki
tī viḻi viḻivan vāḻā
punattuzāy mālaiyānē !
ponni śūz tiruvaraṅgā
enakkini kkati en śollāy
ennai āḻuḍaiya kōvē !

## meaning

Neither I have purity of mind, nor do I utter amicable words. Angrily I am always expressing the burning words of insult (to one and all), without any cause whatsoever. O Lord adorned with fragrant Tulasi garlands! O sacred Lord of Srirangam, girt with the great river Ponni (Kāvēri)! You are the supreme Lord who have myself as your dependent. Kindly tell me, henceforth, what is the way left for me to reach the supreme goal.

## purport

"Na cakṣuṣā gṛhyatē nāpi vācā manasā tu viśuddhēna" (The Supreme Self cannot be apprehended by the eyes or speech; only through a purified mind can He be realized). Śrī Viṣṇu Purāṇam 1.4.41 declares: "yē tu jñānavidhaḥ śuddha cētasastē'khilaṁ jagat | jñānātmakaṁ prapaśyanti tvad rūpaṁ paramēśvara" (O Paramēśvara! Those who realize that the Self is of the nature of knowledge and possess purified minds perceive the entire world as permeated with knowledge and as Your body). Thus, the śāstras affirm that purity of mind is considered a means for experiencing paramātmajñānam (knowledge of the Supreme Self).

The Āḻvār humbly confesses that he lacks even this purity of mind. By saying "ōr tūymai", he indicates that he lacks even the slightest purity one might attain if any of the mind's impurities such as kāmam (lust), kōpam (anger), lōbham (greed), bhayam (fear), dvēṣam (hatred), madam (arrogance), mōham (delusion), and mātsaryam (envy) were diminished. By stating "tūymai illai", he emphasizes that it is not a matter of his mind being occasionally muddled and occasionally pure. Rather, he asserts that he is perpetually devoid of mental purity. There is no purity of mind, granted. But at least, through speech, is there the virtue of uttering sweet words? The Āḻvār laments that he lacks even this. By saying "ōr iṉsol illai", he confesses that he has never inquired about another's well-being, which could be pleasing to their ears. He intimates that even when others inquire about his well-being, he has not responded kindly. Just as Tirumaṅgai Āḻvār said in Periya Tirumoḻi 1.6.5, "neḍuñ collāl maṟutta nīcaṉēṉ" (I am a lowly person who rebuked with harsh words), this Āḻvār echoes the same sentiment. By using "ōr", he hints that even in conversations with those he loves, he uses only harsh words that sear their ears. Thus, with these two phrases, he succinctly expresses what he had elaborated in the previous five pāsurams.
From this point onward, for the remainder of the pāsuram, he speaks of his negative qualities.

Even if he lacked purity of mind and sweetness of speech, others could distance themselves from him and carry on. But he does not stop there. He voluntarily approaches a person of some greatness and, without reason, causes them harm. Initially, he displays all the anger in his mind and looks at them with hostility. "Seṟṟam nōkki" means to look with enmity. Alternatively, since both "seṟṟam" and "siṉam" indicate anger, their combined use here signifies intense anger. The implication is that his fiery gaze would almost cause the other person to collapse under the weight of his wrath. It is not only through looks; even the words he uses would seem like burning embers to the other person, causing them to wither completely. Is the purpose of all this to gain anything?

These actions are not performed in anticipation of any benefit. Looking at someone with anger and scolding them with harsh words become sources of enjoyment in themselves. No other benefit is needed for such actions. The sentiment here is that just as virtuous persons consider doing good to others as inherently beneficial, the Āḻvār regards harming others as inherently rewarding to himself.
Periya Perumāḷ says that since he is such a reprehensible person, there would be no benefit in compelling him. The Āḻvār responds:

Sentient beings cannot abandon Him after recognizing their own lowliness because He has pledged to protect them if they desire Him. He wears a garland of tuḷasī to openly display this quality. The meaning here is that since You are the ordained Lord and after perceiving Your sweetness, how can I let go of You? "Puṉam" refers to the land where the tuḷasī plant flourishes. The tuḷasī on His chest thrives as it would in its native soil, and He wears such a garland. Even if You are a protector, if You are not near me, it will not benefit me. Only to ensure that even the lowliest can attain You, You have come to Śrīraṅgam and assumed a reclining posture here. This is my good fortune, the Āḻvār feels. "Poṉṉi sūḻ" - atop the tuḷasī garland, the holy Kāvēri surrounding Him is like another garland.

To me, with all the lowliness that has been described thus far. Considering my unworthiness, I cannot protect myself, and after entrusting the responsibility of protecting me to You, Please show me the path to my salvation. Even after accumulating all these faults such that it seems impossible to uplift himself, the Āḻvār can still plead his case with Him for upliftment because emperumāṉ is the ordained Lord. "What compels Me to show you the way to your salvation?" asks emperumāṉ. You being the svāmi (Lord) and I being the servitor are eternal states. Hence, who else can protect me? If my ātmavastu (the entity called ātmā) were to degrade, wouldn't the shortcoming due to that be Yours?
