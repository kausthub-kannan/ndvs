## english_scripts

ārttu vaṇḍalambum śōlai
aṇi tiruvaraṅgan tannuḻ
kār ttiraḻ anaiya mēni
kaṇṇanē ! unnai kkāṇum
mārkkam onṟaṟiya māṭṭā
maniśaril turiśanāya
mūrkkanēn vandu ninṟēn
mūrkkanēn mūrkkanēnē

## meaning

Oh Lord Kṛṣṇa of charming beauty like the dense dark rainy clouds! You are dwelling as Śri Ranganatha at Srirangam beautified by the surrounding groves resounding with the humming of honey-bees. I am only a knave among human beings because I do not know the right path to perceive You in the proper perspective. Though I have come (seeking you), nevertheless I am just a wicked, and wicked alone.

## purport

The Āḻvār describes the scene in Śrīraṅgam, where beetles, intoxicated by excess honey from the lush groves, buzz and wander aimlessly. He likens their state to that of muktas (liberated souls) in Paramapada, who sing Sāma Gāna out of sheer joy after experiencing the Lord. This comparison elevates the sweetness of Śrīraṅgam to that of the divine realm.

Drawing a parallel to the Rāmāyaṇa's Sundara Kāṇḍa, where monkeys celebrate in Madhuvana after sighting Sītā, the Āḻvār notes a distinction: while he cries out for existence, the beetles cry out of joy. The phrase "vaṇḍu alambum sōlai" likens the beetles' predicament to Mārkaṇḍeya Maharṣi tossed in the great deluge, emphasizing the overwhelming nature of Śrīraṅgam's beauty.

Śrīraṅgam is described as "aṇi araṅgam", an ornament to saṃsāra. The Āḻvār alludes to Rāmāyaṇa, where a husband is considered a woman's greatest ornament, to highlight Raṅganātha's significance to the world. The Lord's form, "kārttiraḷ anaiya mēni", is compared to rain-laden clouds, not just in color but in qualities of coolness and selfless giving. The term "Kaṇṇan" is used to emphasize His accessibility, contrasting with the distant nature of clouds.

The Āḻvār laments his ignorance of the paths to attain the Lord, despite being born human. He calls himself "thurisanāya" (a thief or cheat), suggesting that compared to him, others might seem like nitya sūris (eternal devotees).
Finally, addressing his audacity in approaching the Lord, the Āḻvār describes himself as "mūrkkan" (a fool), repeating it for emphasis. He acknowledges his ignorance of both the Lord's greatness and his own lowliness, likening his approach to poisoning the pure water enjoyed by the wise. This passage beautifully intertwines devotional fervor with self-deprecation, using rich imagery and scriptural references to convey the Āḻvār's spiritual struggle and deep reverence for Raṅganātha.
