## meaning

Oh Lord Rañganātha! Now the gentle morning breeze from the eastern direction blows, bringing with it the sweet fragrance of flowers from the jasmine (mullai) creepers. The swans have risen from their bed of lotus flowers, shaking off the dew drops from their wide wings. Oh, the Lord of Śrīraṅgam, You have protected the great elephant, Gajēndran, which was in distress after being caught within the massive teeth of the crocodile. Oh Lord! Please wake up and bless us!

## purport

When the Sun rises in the east, the jasmine blossoms growing on creepers intermingle their fragrance with the gentle, blowing wind. The Āḷvār poetically narrates that the wind has penetrated the inner sanctum of Śrīraṅgam, as if the very flowers are offering their prayers at the lotus feet of the Lord. In the early morning, the swans awaken with utmost diligence. The Āḷvār beautifully describes how the male swan rises, delicately removing the morning dew clinging to its wings. Tenderly, this swan then assists its mate, wiping the dew from her wings. The Āḷvār interprets this specific moment of the swan rising and spreading its wings as a herald of morning. Significantly, Bhagavān once appeared as Haṁsa, a swan, to impart divine knowledge to Brahma and Śaṅka Munis. Therefore, just as other swans stir from their slumber, our beloved swan (Raṅganātha) should also awaken. Just as the male swan removes the dew from its wings, Oh Lord, please remove the ignorance from our hearts by bestowing Your divine grace.

The Āḷvār then transitions to the profound narrative of Gajendra, the blessed elephant. A Pāṇḍya king who disregarded Sage Agastya was cursed to become an elephant. The sage, recognizing that the king's momentary lapse stemmed from his deep remembrance of Nārāyaṇa, blessed him with a profound boon: that same divine recollection would ultimately liberate him from his elephantine existence in the hills of Tirukkuṭa. This king was reborn as Gajendra. Concurrently, a Gandharva had been transformed into a crocodile, dwelling in Varuṇa's lotus-filled pond. A sage had previously decreed that the Gandharva would be released from his crocodile birth only upon seizing the leg of the devoted Gajendra. A ferocious struggle ensued for several years, with the crocodile relentlessly attempting to drag the elephant king into the watery depths, while Gajendra desperately tried to free himself from the creature's powerful jaws and sharp teeth. Drawing upon wisdom from his previous birth, Gajendra gathered lotus flowers and began to pray to the eternal protector, Śrīman Nārāyaṇa. Initially, he believed in his own physical prowess to overcome the challenge. However, when he finally realized that no one except the Supreme Lord could save him, his ego and sense of individual identity completely dissolved. At that profound moment of absolute surrender, Gajendra prepared to eulogize the Lord with the sacred verse from Srimad Bhagavatham 8.3.24: "sa vai na devāsura-martya-tiryaṅ na strī na ṣaṇḍho na pumān na jantuḥ nāyaṁ guṇaḥ karma na san na cāsan niṣedha-śeṣo jayatād aśeṣaḥ". This prayer of prapatti translates as: "You are neither deity, demon, nor mortal; neither woman, man, nor creature. You transcend all attributes, actions, and states of being. May that supreme, all-encompassing being appear to protect me!"

As soon as Bhagavān heard Gajendra's prayers, He rushed to protect him without hesitation, hurling His Sudarśana disc at the crocodile, thereby liberating Gajendra. The Āḷvār emphasizes that Gajendra ultimately realized his true spiritual state and jīvātma svarūpa. He understood that liberation and attaining the Divine is not achieved through personal efforts or bhakti yoga, but through the compassionate grace of Bhagavān itself. This profound knowledge liberated him from the cycle of saṁsāra. The Āḷvār implores that Lord of boundless compassion to awaken!
