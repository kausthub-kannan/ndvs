## bio

While the other Āḻvārs sang of their longing for Bhagavān, praised His greatness, or sought refuge at His lotus feet, Periyāḻvār did something extraordinary. He blessed Bhagavān Himself. This singular act earned him a unique place in the history of Vaiṣṇava devotion. In a tradition where devotees seek blessings from the Lord, Periyāḻvār's love was so intense, so maternal, and so protective that he worried not for himself but for Bhagavān. His devotion was not rooted in fear, reverence, or even longing alone. It was rooted in *vātsalya-bhāva*, the affection of a parent toward a beloved child. Through this distinctive mode of devotion, he transformed the relationship between devotee and Bhagavān into one of the most tender and intimate expressions found in Indian religious literature.

Periyāḻvār was born in the sacred town of Śrīvilliputtūr, one of the most celebrated Divya Deśams of the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition. His birth name was Viṣṇucitta, a name that beautifully reflected his nature, for his mind was entirely occupied by thoughts of Viṣṇu. Śrīvilliputtūr, nestled amidst fertile landscapes and temple gardens, would later become one of the most important centres of Vaiṣṇava devotion, owing largely to the lives of Periyāḻvār and his foster daughter Āṇḍāḷ. Tradition reveres Periyāḻvār as an incarnation of Garuḍa, the divine vehicle of Bhagavān Nārāyaṇa. Just as Garuḍa eternally serves the Lord, Viṣṇucitta dedicated every moment of his life to the service of Bhagavān.

Unlike many saints associated with royal courts, philosophical debates, or extensive pilgrimages, Periyāḻvār lived a simple and humble life. His daily occupation was the cultivation of flowers. Maintaining a sacred garden near the temple, he lovingly grew fragrant blossoms which he wove into garlands for Vatapatraśāyī Perumāḷ, the presiding deity of Śrīvilliputtūr. For him, this service was not merely a ritual obligation. Every flower was nurtured as an offering, every garland woven with devotion, and every act of service performed as an expression of love. The temple garden became his world, and the Lord of Śrīvilliputtūr became the centre of his existence.

The defining event in Periyāḻvār's life occurred during the reign of a Pāṇḍya king at Madurai. According to tradition, the king had become deeply interested in understanding the nature of the Supreme Reality. Scholars representing various philosophical and religious traditions gathered in his court, each presenting arguments in support of their respective doctrines. A substantial reward awaited the scholar capable of establishing the identity of the Supreme Being through scriptural authority and reason. Viṣṇucitta, who had never regarded himself as a scholar and had spent his life tending flowers rather than engaging in public debate, received a divine command from Bhagavān instructing him to travel to Madurai and participate in the assembly.

Obedient to the Lord's will, he journeyed to the royal court. There, before learned scholars and assembled dignitaries, the humble gardener expounded the supremacy of Nārāyaṇa using the authority of the Vedas, Upaniṣads, Itihāsas, and Purāṇas. Tradition recounts that divine wisdom flowed effortlessly through him. The scholars were astonished, the king was convinced, and the assembly acknowledged his victory. At that very moment, the reward suspended above the gathering is said to have fallen of its own accord, signifying divine approval. The king honoured Viṣṇucitta and arranged a grand procession through the streets of Madurai, seating him upon a royal elephant amidst great celebration.

It was during this triumphal procession that one of the most celebrated moments in the history of the Āḻvārs occurred. Bhagavān Himself appeared before the assembled crowds, accompanied by Śrī Mahālakṣmī. One might expect a devotee who had just witnessed such a vision to fall into ecstasy, offer praise, or seek blessings. Periyāḻvār did something entirely different. Looking upon the incomparable beauty of the Lord, he was suddenly overcome by concern. What if someone cast an evil eye upon Bhagavān? What if harm somehow came to Him? The thought itself was unbearable. Forgetting that he stood before the Supreme Lord of the universe, he instinctively responded as a loving parent would respond to a precious child. From this overflowing affection emerged the immortal opening of the *Tiruppallāṇḍu*:

*"Pallāṇḍu Pallāṇḍu Pallāyirattaṇḍu..."*

"May You live for many years, for many thousands of years, for countless ages."

The theological significance of this moment is profound. Periyāḻvār did not merely praise Bhagavān; he blessed Him. This inversion of the normal relationship between devotee and deity became the defining characteristic of his devotional outlook. The Lord who protects the universe became, in the eyes of Periyāḻvār, an object of loving concern. Such is the transformative power of *vātsalya-bhakti*. The devotee becomes so absorbed in affection that divine majesty recedes into the background, replaced by intimate love.

Periyāḻvār's literary contributions consist primarily of the *Tiruppallāṇḍu* and the *Periyāḻvār Tirumoḻi*. Together, these works reveal the extraordinary richness of his devotional imagination. Throughout the *Periyāḻvār Tirumoḻi*, he repeatedly assumes the role of Yaśodā, Kṛṣṇa's foster mother, and narrates the Lord's childhood exploits with remarkable tenderness and realism. Few works in devotional literature portray the childhood of Kṛṣṇa with such intimacy. The reader encounters not the cosmic Lord who sustains the universe, but a mischievous child who steals butter, refuses to bathe, plays pranks on neighbours, and fills his mother's life with both delight and anxiety.

These hymns reveal Periyāḻvār's unique genius. He does not merely describe Kṛṣṇa's childhood; he enters into it completely. One moment he is calling the child to take a bath, another moment he is feeding him, comforting him, protecting him, or worrying about his safety. Through these compositions, the infinite Lord becomes accessible in the most human and affectionate manner possible. The theological message underlying these verses is subtle yet profound. The Supreme Being, though transcendent and omnipotent, willingly accepts the love and care of His devotees. Divine greatness is revealed not only through cosmic power but also through divine simplicity.

Another defining chapter in Periyāḻvār's life began with the discovery of a divine child in his garden. One day, while tending the flowers destined for Bhagavān's worship, he found a baby girl beneath a sacred Tulasī plant. Recognizing the event as divinely ordained, he adopted the child and named her Kōdai, known to posterity as Āṇḍāḷ. The relationship between Periyāḻvār and Āṇḍāḷ would become one of the most beloved narratives in the entire Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition. He nurtured her with affection, instructed her in devotion, and raised her amidst the sacred atmosphere of temple service. Little did he realize that the child he had found would one day become one of the greatest poet-saints of India and the eternal bride of Śrī Raṅganātha.

The later years of Periyāḻvār's life were marked by increasing spiritual fulfilment. His devotion deepened through the blossoming of Āṇḍāḷ's own spiritual greatness, and he witnessed firsthand the unfolding of a divine destiny. The humble gardener of Śrīvilliputtūr had become not only a celebrated saint but also the foster father of one of the most revered figures in the Vaiṣṇava tradition.

The influence of Periyāḻvār upon subsequent generations has been immense. His hymns established *vātsalya-bhāva* as one of the most cherished modes of devotion within Vaiṣṇavism. Through his poetry, countless devotees learned to approach Bhagavān not merely with reverence but with affection. The image of the devotee blessing the Lord remains one of the most distinctive and moving themes in the entire Bhakti tradition.

Today, Periyāḻvār is remembered as the saint whose love transcended all conventional boundaries. Standing before the Lord of the universe, he did not ask for wealth, liberation, or even divine grace. Instead, he prayed for Bhagavān's well-being. In that moment, the gardener of Śrīvilliputtūr revealed a profound spiritual truth: the highest devotion is not concerned with what one receives from Bhagavān, but with loving Him so completely that one forgets oneself entirely. Through his life, his hymns, and his boundless affection, Periyāḻvār continues to inspire generations of devotees to cultivate a love for Bhagavān that is selfless, intimate, and eternal.
