## bio

Following the appearance of Poigai Āḻvār, the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition reverently remembers Bhūtat Āḻvār as the second among the *Mudal Āḻvārs*. If Poigai Āḻvār transformed the universe into a lamp with which to behold the Supreme Lord, Bhūtat Āḻvār illuminated that lamp with the fuel of divine love. Together with Poigai Āḻvār and Pey Āḻvār, he occupies a unique place in the history of Tamil Bhakti, standing at the very beginning of a devotional movement that would eventually reshape the religious life of South India.

Bhūtat Āḻvār is traditionally associated with the sacred coastal town of Tirukkaḍalmallai, known today as Mahābalipuram. Situated on the shores of the Bay of Bengal, this ancient settlement was already an important centre of trade and culture during the Pallava period. Long before its rock-cut monuments earned fame among historians, it was celebrated by devotees as a sacred abode of Bhagavān.

According to the Guruparamparā tradition, Bhūtat Āḻvār did not take birth through ordinary human means. He manifested within a fragrant *kurukkatti* flower that bloomed in a sacred tank near the Divya Deśam of Sthalasayana Perumāḷ. Just as Poigai Āḻvār emerged from a lotus, Bhūtat Āḻvār too appeared as a divine child through miraculous circumstances, emphasizing his transcendental nature.

Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition identifies him as the incarnation of Bhagavān's divine mace, the *Kaumodakī Gadā*. In the iconography of Viṣṇu, the mace symbolizes strength, protection, and the destruction of ignorance. The life and hymns of Bhūtat Āḻvār reflect these very qualities. His devotion was powerful, unwavering, and capable of dispelling spiritual darkness.

The name "Bhūtat" often invites curiosity. In ordinary Tamil usage, the word may evoke the idea of a supernatural being. Within the Āḻvār tradition, however, the name carries a different significance. It signifies one who has become completely possessed by devotion to Bhagavān. Every thought, emotion, and action of Bhūtat Āḻvār was centred upon Nārāyaṇa. His individuality had become inseparable from divine love.

Like the other early Āḻvārs, Bhūtat Āḻvār is described as possessing innate spiritual wisdom. He did not acquire realization through prolonged scholastic study. Rather, knowledge of the Supreme Lord arose naturally within him, as though it were being remembered rather than learned. This feature is common to the lives of the Āḻvārs, who are regarded not merely as saints but as divinely inspired beings sent to rekindle devotion in the world.

The most celebrated event in Bhūtat Āḻvār's life occurred at Tirukkōvilūr. During a stormy night, Poigai Āḻvār had already taken shelter in the narrow vestibule of Mṛkaṇḍu Ṛṣi's āśrama. Bhūtat Āḻvār arrived shortly thereafter and requested refuge from the rain. Though the space was limited, the two saints accommodated one another.

Soon Pey Āḻvār also arrived, and the three stood together within the tiny enclosure. As they stood in darkness, they suddenly became aware of an additional presence among them. The confined space seemed crowded beyond what was physically possible. Yet the atmosphere was filled not with discomfort but with overwhelming spiritual ecstasy.

Poigai Āḻvār responded by creating a cosmic lamp through his opening verse. Bhūtat Āḻvār, inspired by the same divine experience, composed the first verse of the *Irandām Tiruvandādi*. His imagery is among the most beautiful in all devotional literature:

*"Anbē tagaḷiyā, ārvamē neyyāga..."*

"Let love be the lamp, let longing be the ghee."

The contrast with Poigai Āḻvār's vision is striking. The first Āḻvār had used the external universe as a lamp. Bhūtat Āḻvār turned inward. For him, the lamp was not the earth, nor the oceans, nor the sun. The lamp was love itself. The fuel was yearning. The flame was spiritual awareness directed toward Bhagavān.

This verse reveals the essential character of Bhūtat Āḻvār's spirituality. His devotion was intensely personal and emotional. While deeply grounded in theological truth, it was expressed through the language of love. The path to Bhagavān was not merely intellectual understanding but heartfelt longing.

Bhūtat Āḻvār's literary contribution consists of one hundred verses known as the *Irandām Tiruvandādi*. Like the work of Poigai Āḻvār, it is composed in the *andādi* style, where each verse flows into the next through an interconnected chain of words and ideas. The structure itself symbolizes the unbroken remembrance of Bhagavān.

Throughout the work, Bhūtat Āḻvār repeatedly emphasizes the supremacy of Nārāyaṇa and the transformative power of devotion. His hymns portray Bhagavān not as an abstract philosophical principle but as a living presence who enters the hearts of devotees. The Lord becomes both the object of love and the source from which that love arises.

A notable feature of Bhūtat Āḻvār's poetry is its emotional intensity. The language often conveys the urgency of a devotee who cannot bear separation from the Divine. Love is not presented as a metaphor. It is treated as a genuine spiritual force capable of illuminating reality itself.

His verses also reveal a profound appreciation for the manifestations of Bhagavān in various sacred temples. The Divya Deśams are not merely locations in geography; they are places where the transcendent Lord becomes accessible to human beings. Through his hymns, Bhūtat Āḻvār helped establish the sacred landscape that would later become central to Śrīvaiṣṇava pilgrimage.

The influence of Bhūtat Āḻvār extends beyond his own literary work. Together with Poigai Āḻvār and Pey Āḻvār, he established the pattern that would characterize the entire Āḻvār movement. Their hymns demonstrated that profound spiritual truths could be communicated through Tamil poetry without sacrificing philosophical depth. This achievement opened the doors of devotion to countless devotees who may never have had access to formal Sanskrit learning.

In later centuries, Śrīvaiṣṇava Ācāryas frequently cited the opening verse of the *Irandām Tiruvandādi* as a perfect summary of the devotional path. Knowledge may guide the seeker, discipline may strengthen the seeker, but it is love that ultimately illuminates the presence of Bhagavān.

Today, Bhūtat Āḻvār is remembered as the saint whose lamp was kindled by devotion itself. If Poigai Āḻvār provided the lamp and Pey Āḻvār would soon behold the divine vision revealed by its light, Bhūtat Āḻvār supplied the fuel that made illumination possible. His life teaches that the deepest spiritual insight arises not merely from learning or austerity, but from a heart overflowing with love for the Supreme Lord.
