## bio

Āḻavandār, whose given name was Yamunaitturaivan and who is also known as Yāmunācārya, was the grandson of Nāthamunigaḷ and the great teacher who carried the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition forward to Rāmānuja. According to the traditional account, he was born in Vīranārāyaṇapuram, an agrahāram on the banks of the Kāveri, more than a thousand years ago. His father was Īśvaramuni, the son of Nāthamunigaḷ. During a pilgrimage to the north, Nāthamunigaḷ had been drawn by the grace of Lord Kṛṣṇa, the Yamunaitturaivan, on the banks of the Yamunā at Vṛndāvana, and he wished to name the child born to his son after that Lord. The boy was therefore named Yamunaitturaivan, and as he grew into a renowned ācārya he came to be called Āḻavandār ("the one who came to rule") and Yāmunācārya.

As a boy he studied the śāstras under Mahābhāṣya Bhaṭṭar. At that time the king's court scholar, Akkiyāḻvān, had the habit of demanding a tax of one tenth of the earnings of other scholars, as a king taxes his subjects. When Akkiyāḻvān sent a demand to Bhaṭṭar through the king's servants, the teacher was deeply troubled. The young Yamunaitturaivan took the letter, tore it to shreds, and sent back a verse in reply declaring that they were not merely poets or masters of one system, but scholars able to humble the pride of any rival, like lions subduing a rutting elephant. The king and queen heard of this challenge. The queen judged that whoever answered so boldly must be a great scholar, and the king, without telling Akkiyāḻvān, sent his own palanquin to invite the boy to court. Yamunaitturaivan accepted, and entered the court reciting two verses of proud confidence in his learning of the two Mīmāṃsās.

When he arrived, the queen, struck by his radiance, said that his appearance alone proclaimed his greatness and that the child would win. The king replied that Akkiyāḻvān would prevail, and said that if the boy won he would give half his kingdom. The queen vowed in turn that she would become the king's servant if Akkiyāḻvān won. Akkiyāḻvān, looking down on the boy's youth, proposed a debate on worldly matters, in which he would refute whatever was said. Yamunaitturaivan posed three statements: that Akkiyāḻvān's mother was barren, that the king was the emperor of the whole world, and that the queen was chaste. Akkiyāḻvān could not reject them without insulting himself or the royal couple, and so he fell silent. The boy then gave the answers himself. The first rested on the saying that a crow and a banana plant are called barren because each bears only once, and that Akkiyāḻvān too was an only son. The second rested on the point that a true emperor must rule the whole earth bounded by the four oceans, which the Chola king did not. The third rested on the Vedic marriage mantras that name Soma, Gandharva, and Agni before the human husband. These were skilful, harmless replies that offended no one. The queen, delighted, exclaimed whether this was the one who had come to rule over her (_āḷavantīrō_). The king gave him half his kingdom as promised, and from that day he was called Āḻavandār. Some years later, he had four sons: Coṭṭai Nambi, Deivattukkarasu Nambi, Piḷḷaiyarasu Nambi, and Tiruvaraṅga Perumāḷ.

Nāthamunigaḷ had asked Uyyakkoṇḍār to teach the boy, but Uyyakkoṇḍār passed the task to his disciple Maṇakkāl Nambi. Nambi won access to the king by bringing him his favourite spinach, and then taught him the inner meaning of the Bhagavad Gītā and the _carama śloka_. Āḻavandār regretted having spent his life without knowledge of the true principles, and when Nambi led him to Śrīraṅgam and showed him Lord Raṅganātha as the treasure his ancestors had left for him, he gave up the kingdom, took sannyāsa, and settled in Śrīraṅgam to teach. Fifteen disciples took refuge at his feet, among them Periya Nambi, Tirukkōṭṭiyūr Nambi, Tirumālaiyāṇḍān, Tirumalai Nambi, Tirumōkūr Nambi, Deivavāriyāṇḍān, Mārane Nambi, and Āḷ Koṇḍi.

His life is also linked to Rāmānuja, whom he never taught in person. Having heard that Rāmānuja was studying under Yādavaprakāśa in Kāñcīpuram and had disagreed with him on the meaning of a Upaniṣadic passage, he travelled there and prayed to Lord Varadarāja for Rāmānuja's welfare. He saw Rāmānuja from a distance, among Yādavaprakāśa's students, and blessed him with a verse from his own _Stotraratna_. He recited the verse beginning _yasya prasādakalayā_, by whose grace the deaf hear, the lame run, the dumb speak, the blind see, and the barren bear children. He asked that Rāmānuja become the guardian of the Viśiṣṭādvaita tradition. He did not want to interrupt Rāmānuja's study of rival systems, so he returned to Śrīraṅgam without speaking to him.

When Āḻavandār fell ill, he sent his chief disciple Periya Nambi to Kāñcī to bring Rāmānuja. Meanwhile, he gave his last teaching to his students, who had asked for the deepest advice on surrender (_prapatti_). He told them to seek the grace of a true ācārya, to surrender to the Lord with faith, to live piously in constant remembrance of the lineage of teachers, and to perform the obligatory (_ājñā_) and permitted (_anujñā_) services without fail. He then asked for the sandals (_pādukā_) of Nāthamunigaḷ, worshipped them, and took their sacred water. As he grew weaker, he told his disciples of three wishes he had been unable to fulfil: to honour the sages Parāśara and Vyāsa by giving their names to future children, to write a commentary on the Brahma Sūtras in accord with Viśiṣṭādvaita, and to write a commentary on Nammāḻvār's _Tiruvāymoḻi_. As he spoke, three fingers of his hand folded. At the auspicious hour, he recited verses of the _Tiruvāymoḻi_, sat in the lotus posture, placed the sandals of Nāthamunigaḷ on his head, recited the names of Maṇakkāl Nambi and Uyyakkoṇḍār and the _dvaya mantra_, and ascended to Śrī Vaikuṇṭham. His body was honoured with the Lord's garlands and offerings and taken in procession to the bank of the Kollidam for the final rites.

Periya Nambi and Rāmānuja were rushing toward Śrīraṅgam when they saw a great crowd at the bathing ghat and learned that Āḻavandār had passed away. Rāmānuja paid homage to the body from feet to head and noticed three fingers folded. When the disciples explained the three unfulfilled wishes, he vowed, with folded hands, that by the grace of Lord Varadarāja he would fulfil them, and the three fingers straightened. The disciples, moved, saw this as a sign that Rāmānuja would be the establisher of the tradition.

Āḻavandār left eight works: the _Āgamaprāmāṇya_, _Puruṣanirṇaya_, _Ātmasiddhi_, _Saṃvitsiddhi_, _Īśvarasiddhi_, _Gītārtha Saṅgraha_, _Catuḥślokī_, and the _Stotraratna_. The _Stotraratna_ and the _Gītārtha Saṅgraha_ remain especially beloved in the Śrīvaiṣṇava community. He is traditionally revered as the great link between the early ācāryas, from Nāthamunigaḷ through Maṇakkāl Nambi, and Rāmānuja, who completed his unfinished wishes.
