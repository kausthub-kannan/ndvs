## bio

Śrīmad Ādivaṇ Śaṭakōpan occupies a distinctive place in the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition as the founder and first pontiff of the Ahōbila Maṭha. Tradition holds that Lord Malōlan of Ahōbilam appeared in the dream of Kiḍāmbi Śrīnivāsācār and instructed him to come to the temple town, and that from this divine summons grew a lineage of ascetics who have carried the worship of Lord Malōlan across the land for more than six centuries.

Bearing Nammāḻvār’s own name, Śaṭakōpan, he longed to visit Āḻvārtirunagari. There, he found that Nammāḻvār’s temple required restoration and that the Āḻvār’s image was nowhere to be seen. He camped there and preached the doctrine of _prapatti_, reciting Maturakavi’s verses in his distress. Nammāḻvār then appeared in a vision and told him that his image lay in a riverbed beside a ravine, and that the Jīyar should recover and restore it. A Pāṇḍya king, hunting nearby, was drawn to the Jīyar’s presence, fell at his feet and sought to become a Vaiṣṇava. Together they went to the difficult spot, and as the Jīyar prayed, the image slowly rose from the riverbed and settled in his arms, as Malōlan had done before. The king assisted in the restoration of Nammāḻvār’s temple and the reinstatement of his image. The Maṭha’s account names a reigning chief, Kulaśēkhara Pāṇḍya, but concedes that his identity is uncertain. The king built _maṇḍapas_ and arranged for worship, and these structures stand on the banks of the Tāmiraparaṇi, along with a shrine for Vedānta Deśika that the Jīyar built. The king so revered him that he had an image of the Jīyar installed in the Nammāḻvār shrine, and Nammāḻvār is said to have given him the name Ādi (first), Vaṇ (powerful), Śaṭakōpan. The Maṭha’s introduction preserves a variant: that Lord Āttippirāṉ of Āḻvārtirunagari added “Vaṇ” and Nammāḻvār added “Ādi”. Either way, it is the origin of the name by which he is remembered, and his successors have borne the prefix “Vaṇ Śaṭakōpa”.

The Jīyar then went to the Vijayanagara kingdom, whose ruler received him with full honours and gave him grants and presents. It was at this time that Maṇappākkam Tōḻappar became his disciple, studied _Śrībhāṣya_, _Bhagavad Viṣaya_ and _Rahasyatrayasāra_ at his feet, and wrote authoritative works on various _śāstras_ at his bidding.

He built a maṭha at both Tirumalai and Tirupati and consecrated images of Lakṣmīnarasiṁha in each. He also provided steps for climbing the Tirumalai hills and built several _maṇḍapas_ along the way, so that pilgrims could rest on the steep ascent. At Kāñcīpuram he built a thousand-pillared _maṇḍapa_ in the Varadarāja temple, where a pillar still shows an image of Lakṣmīnarasiṁha with a figure of the Jīyar on the pillar opposite. Tradition says it was built from the wealth surrendered by a scholar, Udayabhānu Miśra, who lost to him in a debate lasting nearly a fortnight. At the northern entrance of the temple, he built his maṭha and installed an image of Lakṣmīnarasiṁha and one of himself.

His work at Śrīraṅgam was the most extensive. He was received there with the honours due to a prince among ascetics. He renovated the seven _prākāra_ walls. On divine command, he built a frontal _maṇḍapa_ at the Daśāvatāra shrine, which Tirumaṅgai Āḻvār had founded, along with a shrine for Vedānta Deśika in front of the Śrīraṅga Nācciyār shrine.

After sixty years of service in _sannyāsa_, he returned in old age to his birthplace, Melkōṭe, and attained _paramapada_ there in April 1458. His Bṛndāvanam at Melkōṭe remains a place of devotion for his followers. He was succeeded by the eminent Nampākkam Svāmi, Śrīvaṇ Śaṭakōpa Śrīman Nārāyaṇa Yatīndra Mahādēśikan. The line he began has not broken. To this day, it continues as an unbroken succession of forty-six ācāryas, tracing its founding to 1398 CE at Ahōbilam in Andhra Pradesh. The Ahōbila Maṭha today runs schools, supports Vedic learning, and maintains centres in India and abroad. All of it rests on the moment in which a young scholar from Melkōṭe set out for Ahōbilam in answer to a dream. Ādivaṇ Śaṭakōpan is remembered not only as an ascetic but also as an institution-builder, and his life illustrates how a single act of surrender can give rise to a tradition that outlasts empires.
