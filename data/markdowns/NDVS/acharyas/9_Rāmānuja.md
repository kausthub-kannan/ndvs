## bio

In the long line of ācāryas of the Śrīvaiṣṇava tradition, none has shone with the brilliance of Rāmānuja, also called Emperumānār, Udaiyavar, Yatirāja, Ilaiyāḻvār, and Śrībhāṣyakārar. He was not the originator of the siddhānta, which the tradition holds to be the true essence of the Vedas. But he was its backbone, and his contribution to it was complete in every way. He is traditionally revered as an incarnation of Ādi Śeṣa, the Lord's divine serpent-servant, who had been Lakṣmaṇa in the Rāmāyaṇa and Balarāma in the age of Kṛṣṇa. He was born at Bhūtapurī, today's Śrīperumbudūr near Kāñcī, to Āsūri Keśava Somayāji and Kāntimatī. From the beginning he was a brilliant student, and his boldness before his Advaitic teacher, Yādavaprakāśa, is well remembered. When Yādavaprakāśa gave a forced and unseemly reading of the Upaniṣadic phrase describing the Lord's eyes, Rāmānuja could not accept it. He gave the graceful meaning, that they are beautiful as freshly blossomed lotuses, and showed that he would not trade devotion and the plain sense of the text for clever word-play. Further disagreements followed, and he finally parted from his teacher and found his true place in the Vaiṣṇava tradition.

Though he never met Āḻavandār (Yāmunācārya) in person, Rāmānuja inherited his legacy. When Āḻavandār passed away, three fingers of his hand remained curled, a sign of three unfulfilled wishes: to write a commentary on the Brahma Sūtras, to honour the sage Parāśara, and to spread the doctrine of surrender to the Lord. Standing before the sacred body, Rāmānuja vowed to fulfil them, and the tradition says the fingers straightened. He received the sacred teachings from the great ācāryas Periya Nambi (Mahāpūrṇa) and Tirukkōṣṭiyūr Nambi (Goṣṭhipūrṇa), and then took sannyāsa as Yatirāja, the king of ascetics.

Āḻavandār's wish for a commentary on the Brahma Sūtras in the light of Viśiṣṭādvaita rested on an ancient commentary by the sage Bodhāyana. This _Bodhāyana Vṛtti_ was a commentary on the Brahma Sūtras of Bādarāyaṇa (Vyāsa), and Rāmānuja knew that his own work must stand on that foundation rather than on fresh invention. The text was not available in the south and was preserved in Kashmir, so he set out on the long journey north with his devoted disciple Kūreśa (Kūrattāḻvān). According to tradition, the scholars there were reluctant to part with the manuscript. Rāmānuja was allowed to study it, and Kūreśa followed it so closely that he committed it to memory. The Goddess Śāradā is said to have been pleased with Rāmānuja's interpretation of a passage of the _Chāndogya Upaniṣad_ and to have honoured him with the title _Śrībhāṣyakāra_. On the return journey, the manuscript was taken back by those unwilling to see it travel south, and Kūreśa's memory saved the work. Rāmānuja dictated while Kūreśa recalled, and in this way the _Śrībhāṣya_ took shape. Kūreśa was careful to note wherever Rāmānuja's own words diverged from the _Vṛtti_, so that the commentary stayed true to the tradition it served. Alongside the _Śrībhāṣya_ he composed the _Vedārtha Saṅgraha_, _Vedānta Dīpa_, and _Vedānta Sāra_, as well as the _Gītā Bhāṣya_ and the _Gadyatrayam_, a trio of devotional prose works on surrender.

What defined Rāmānuja was not only his scholarship but his compassion. He saw that the longing for liberation and the wish to serve Nārāyaṇa belong to no one birth or station. Wherever he found a sincere desire to surrender to the Lord, he made sure it was met with proper initiation. He did this in the way the tradition prescribes, through a living ācārya who gives the _pañca saṃskāra_ (the five sacraments) and the sacred mantras together with due instruction. In this way the teaching reached the disciple as a lived discipline and not merely as words heard. It was this boundless compassion, his eagerness to open the path of liberation to all who sincerely sought it, that earned him the title _Emperumānār_ ("our Lord," "our great master"), a name of deep affection given by those who felt his care.

His journeys took him across the land, from established centres like Kāñcī and Śrīraṅgam to hill shrines like Tirumala and the difficult pilgrimage to Sāligrāma. Along the way he built what was, without doubt, one of the most robust systems of temple administration of the early twelfth century, and he applied it to Vaiṣṇava temples throughout the country. At Śrīraṅgam he organised the temple's worship and administration and drew thousands of disciples. When persecution forced him to leave, he spent years in Melkote in Karnataka, where the Hoysala king welcomed him and he restored the temple of Tirunārāyaṇa. There he embraced communities long kept at the margins of temple life and arranged for their participation in worship and service. He taught that a devotee's worth lay in devotion, humility, and service to the Lord and His devotees, not in birth. In this he joined the depth of a philosopher to the tenderness of a true ācārya, and his vision of a community open to all who seek refuge in Nārāyaṇa remains among his most enduring gifts.

Rāmānuja is said to have lived 120 years and to have attained the Lord's abode at Śrīraṅgam in 1137 CE. His teaching, Viśiṣṭādvaita, upholds the reality of individual souls and of the world as the body of the Lord, and it remains a living tradition to this day. He is remembered as the great fulfiller of Āḻavandār's wishes, the builder of the school that still bears his name, and the ācārya whose compassion reached every seeker.
