## bio

Uyyakkoṇḍār, whose given name was Puṇḍarīkākṣa, was the chief disciple of Nāthamunigaḷ and the second great teacher in the Śrīvaiṣṇava Ācārya lineage. According to the traditional account, he was born in the sacred place of Tiruvellarai, near Śrīraṅgam, in the Kali-yuga year 3987 (Prabhava), in the month of Chittirai under the Kṛttikā star. He is revered as an incarnation of Jayatsena, one of the chieftains in the retinue of Viṣvaksena, the commander-in-chief of Lord Nārāyaṇa's celestial army. He was one of eight disciples who gathered around Nāthamunigaḷ at Vīranārāyaṇapuram, the others being Kurugai Kāvalappan, Nambi Karuṇākara Dāsar, Ēru Tiruvuḍaiyār, Tirukkaṇṇamaṅgaiyāṇḍān, Vānamādeviyāṇḍān, Uruppattūr Āccānpiḷḷai, and Cokattūrāḻvān. To them the master taught the sacred mantras, their inner meanings, the Divya Prabandham, and its special interpretive points. Among them, Puṇḍarīkākṣa stood foremost in learning, devotion, and humility.

His life is remembered above all for one defining episode. When Nāthamunigaḷ's father-in-law, Vaṅgīpuratt-Ācchi, asked that his daughter Aravindappāvai be brought to his home, Nāthamunigaḷ sent her under the escort of Puṇḍarīkākṣa. After he had brought her safely there and asked leave to return, Ācchi invited him to eat first. But some mischievous members of the household told Ācchi that it was improper to feed him inside the house. He was therefore made to sit outside and was served only the previous day's rice soaked in water. Puṇḍarīkākṣa took it without complaint and counted it as a mark of good fortune, reasoning that it was the leftover food of devotees (_bhāgavata śeṣam_), which purifies and leads to a good end. When he returned to Vīranārāyaṇapuram, Nāthamunigaḷ saw the contentment on his face and, through his yogic insight, understood what had happened. On hearing the account he was overjoyed, embraced him, and said _"nammai uyyakkoṇḍīr"_ ("you have redeemed us"). From that day Puṇḍarīkākṣa was known as Uyyakkoṇḍār. When some bystanders objected that the scriptures forbid eating food cooked the day before, Nāthamunigaḷ answered, citing Manu and other dharma texts, that such rules concern particular conditions and would not be violated in the homes of the learned and one's own kin. Ācchi, on learning of the incident, was ashamed, scolded his household, apologized to Uyyakkoṇḍār, and left his daughter with Nāthamunigaḷ. He took the matter as a blessing, since a daughter given to a knower of the Vedas is said to uplift ten generations of her father's line.

Nāthamunigaḷ taught the science of yoga to Kurugaikkāvalappan, but appointed Uyyakkoṇḍār his successor for teaching the Vedas and Vedānta to all his disciples. In keeping with this charge, Uyyakkoṇḍār taught Maṇakkāl Nambi (Rāmamiśra), Tiruvallikkēṇi Pāṇ Perumāḷaraiyar, Cettalūr Cendalaṅkāradāsar, Puṇḍarīkadāsar, and Ulagaperumāḷ Naṅgai. Before his passing, Nāthamunigaḷ had asked him to instruct his grandson Yamunaitturaivan (later Āḻavandār, or Yāmunācārya) in the Śrīvaiṣṇava teaching. Uyyakkoṇḍār was willing, but he feared that his own life might end before the child came of age for such study. He therefore passed the task to his principal disciple, Maṇakkāl Nambi, who waited for the right time and eventually taught Āḻavandār. Through this arrangement the teaching passed unbroken from Nāthamunigaḷ to Uyyakkoṇḍār, to Maṇakkāl Nambi, to Āḻavandār, and then to Rāmānuja. The tradition also recalls the affection between teacher and student in this line. Once, when Maṇakkāl Nambi said at his teacher's feet that serving him was all he desired, Uyyakkoṇḍār was pleased and blessed him. Uyyakkoṇḍār is not known to have composed any written works. His contribution lay in preserving and handing on, faithfully and without alteration, what he had received, and in showing by his own conduct that reverence for fellow devotees is itself the highest qualification. He is traditionally revered as a vital link between Nāthamunigaḷ and the later Ācāryas of the Śrīvaiṣṇava sampradāya.
